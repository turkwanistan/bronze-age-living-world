# Bronze Age Simulation Encyclopedia

- **Research question:** What is known, reasonably inferred, and seriously theorized about Bronze Age societies, environments, institutions, everyday life, culture, behavior, and interregional interaction, with enough detail to build a historically grounded character-and-world simulation?
- **Generated:** 2026-08-28
- **Scope:** Global but simulation-centered. The main chronological focus is roughly 3300–1000 BCE across Eurasia and North Africa, with region-specific chronologies and selective treatment of later Bronze Age sequences where the term begins later. The report emphasizes societies for which archaeological, textual, bioarchaeological, archaeogenetic, environmental, and economic evidence can constrain simulation behavior.

---

## Executive conclusions

1. **There was no single Bronze Age social system or Bronze Age personality.** “Bronze Age” is a technological-archaeological label whose dates and social meaning differ radically by region. A simulation should never give “Bronze Age people” one psychological template. The correct unit is a person embedded in a household, settlement, political economy, ecology, language community, cult, legal status, profession, and relationship network.

2. **The household was usually more fundamental than the autonomous individual.** Kin obligations, marriage alliances, inheritance, household property, dependents, patronage, and local reputation constrained choices. Individuals could be ambitious, affectionate, resentful, entrepreneurial, skeptical, violent, generous, fearful, devout, or opportunistic, but their feasible actions were often filtered through family and status obligations.

3. **The best evidence for human behavior comes from combining unlike sources.** Palaces and tombs exaggerate elites; administrative tablets exaggerate institutions; letters and lawsuits disproportionately preserve literate groups; burials may encode ideology rather than ordinary life; isotopes and ancient DNA reveal movement, diet, and biological kinship but not identity by themselves. A good simulation therefore uses evidence-weighted distributions rather than treating any one source class as a complete social portrait.

4. **Bronze Age worlds were far more connected than a map of separate civilizations suggests.** Copper, tin, silver, gold, lapis lazuli, ivory, timber, textiles, wine, oil, animals, glass, prestige vessels, technologies, marriage partners, mercenaries, migrants, merchants, diplomats, and ideas crossed enormous distances. The Uluburun wreck is a vivid Late Bronze Age snapshot: roughly ten tonnes of copper and about one tonne of tin accompanied by luxury goods and culturally diverse objects, demonstrating that elite and commercial networks could integrate distant production zones. The Old Assyrian archives likewise document organized donkey caravans carrying tin and textiles between Mesopotamia and Anatolia. [R01][R02]

5. **Bronze itself is a network technology.** Tin bronze normally requires copper and tin from different geological zones, plus miners, charcoal, smelters, alloying expertise, casting, smithing, transport, measurement, and institutions capable of moving or exchanging surplus. That does not mean every bronze object came through a centralized state system; household, itinerant, elite, temple/palace, and merchant production could coexist. In simulation terms, metal supply should be a chain with multiple failure points, substitutes, recycling, and political chokepoints.

6. **Status, reciprocity, and reputation are unusually important behavioral variables.** Old Babylonian letters show men of an elite/common-notable status category creating class identity through the language of brothers, friends, and colleagues—and expressing anxiety when expected reciprocity failed. Amarna diplomacy shows “brother” kings bargaining intensely over gifts, marriages, recognition, and parity. Deir el-Medina letters and lawsuits reveal family quarrels, inheritance disputes, gossip, work complaints, affection, neglect, and practical bargaining. These are better foundations for agent psychology than stereotypes such as “honor culture.” [R03][R04][R05]

7. **Religion was not a separate Sunday-like activity.** Divine, ancestral, ritual, legal, medical, agricultural, political, and household concerns overlapped. Weather, illness, childbirth, war, dreams, pollution, omens, sacrifice, ancestors, and divine displeasure could be treated as causally connected to material events. A character may still doubt a particular omen, priest, or diagnosis; the simulation should distinguish belief in a supernatural order from confidence in a specific interpreter.

8. **Gender systems were patriarchal in many documented settings but not socially uniform and not equivalent to female powerlessness.** Women could own or control property, inherit, litigate, conduct business, hold religious offices, manage estates, organize textile production, transmit or embody alliances, and sometimes exercise political power. Residence rules and inheritance differed even among contemporary European communities: Leubingen appears predominantly patrilineal/virilocal with female exogamy, while Mokrin shows female exogamy without strict patrilocality and evidence that women could inherit status. El Argar provides another strongly patrilineal/virilocal case. [R06][R07][R08]

9. **Coercion existed on a spectrum.** Chattel-like slavery, household slavery, debt dependency, corvée, attached labor, captive labor, military levies, temple/palace dependents, tenancy, clientage, and obligations to chiefs or kings should not be collapsed into a binary free/slave variable. In Old Babylonian contexts, debt and credit could produce temporary or negotiable forms of bondage; in other contexts war captives could be permanently alienated. Mycenaean texts distinguish dependent categories, but the legal and lived meanings remain partly obscure.

10. **Environmental shocks matter through institutions rather than as deterministic collapse buttons.** Drought, flood, salinization, epidemic disease, pasture failure, deforestation, crop disease, harbor change, and earthquake could be devastating, but effects depended on storage, crop diversity, irrigation, mobility, trade, political extraction, inequality, conflict, and local ecology. The once-popular “global 4.2 ka megadrought” model is now strongly challenged by a 2024 synthesis of 1,142 paleoclimate datasets, which found no globally coherent exceptional event at that time. Regional climate crises remain real. [R09]

11. **The Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE was a transformation, not one simultaneous apocalypse.** Mycenaean palace systems, the Hittite state, Ugarit, and other institutions failed or transformed on different schedules; Egypt survived with reduced reach; Cyprus and the Levant show both destruction and continuity; some rural economies persisted. Climate stress, warfare, internal political conflict, migration, maritime raiding, disrupted trade, disease hypotheses, earthquakes, and elite fragility have all been proposed. Current high-quality scholarship favors interacting, regionally variable causes rather than one universal trigger. [R10][R11][R12]

12. **For simulation, culture should alter priors and constraints, not dictate destiny.** Each character should have individual traits, but the environment sets costs and expectations: whom they trust, what counts as shameful, what obligations are enforceable, who can own land, how marriage is negotiated, which gods matter, how news travels, whether literacy is available, and how much coercive reach the state has.

# Part I — What “the Bronze Age” means

## 1. A regional, not universal, chronology

The Bronze Age is not a worldwide synchronized age. It is most useful where bronze metallurgy, associated exchange networks, and related changes in social complexity became archaeologically important. In some regions it is embedded in long traditions of urban states; elsewhere it describes chiefly societies with little or no writing. Much of sub-Saharan Africa did not pass through a distinct archaeological Bronze Age at all; iron metallurgy emerged without a preceding bronze-dominant phase in many areas. Mainland Southeast Asia enters what archaeologists call a Bronze Age much later than Mesopotamia or the Aegean. [R13]

Approximate simulation chronologies:

| Region | Useful Bronze Age framing | Important caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Mesopotamia / Syria | c. 3300–1200 BCE, subdivided Early/Middle/Late | Urbanism and writing predate widespread tin bronze; political periods are more meaningful than metal alone. |
| Anatolia | c. 3000–1200 BCE | Strong regional diversity; Hittite imperial period belongs mainly to Late Bronze Age. |
| Egypt | Early Dynastic/Old Kingdom through New Kingdom overlaps c. 3100–1070 BCE | Egyptian chronology is usually dynastic, not “Bronze Age.” |
| Aegean | c. 3000–1050 BCE | Cycladic, Minoan, Helladic/Mycenaean sequences differ. |
| Levant/Cyprus | c. 3300–1200/1050 BCE | Egypt, Syria, Anatolia, and maritime exchange strongly shape local phases. |
| Iranian plateau / Gulf | c. 3300–1200 BCE, highly regional | Elam, plateau societies, Dilmun, Magan and coastal communities differ greatly. |
| Indus / South Asia | Mature Harappan c. 2600–1900 BCE within a longer Early/Late sequence | Bronze mattered, but the Indus system cannot be understood as a metal-driven analogue of Mesopotamia. |
| Central Asia | c. 2300–1500 BCE for BMAC core; steppe Bronze Age broader | Sedentary oasis, agro-pastoral, and mobile communities overlap. |
| China | Erlitou c. 1900–1500; Shang c. 1600–1046 BCE; regional Bronze traditions | “Xia” identification of Erlitou remains debated; Sanxingdui is a distinct southwest tradition. |
| Central/Western/Northern Europe | often c. 2500/2200–800/500 BCE depending region | Many non-state societies; archaeological “cultures” should not be treated as ethnic nations. |
| Mainland Southeast Asia | broadly late 2nd millennium to mid-1st millennium BCE | Begins after the core Near Eastern Bronze Age has ended. |

### Simulation rule
Use **absolute year + local period label**. Never allow a global `bronze_age_phase = late` variable to imply synchronized institutions everywhere.

## 2. Geography as social structure

Bronze Age social worlds are partly geography converted into incentives.

### River floodplains
Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus zone, the Yellow River basin, and the Chengdu Plain could support dense populations and surplus, but required different responses to water. The Nile's relatively predictable inundation differs from Mesopotamian irrigation and salinity risks; Indus urban water systems differ again; northern Chinese millet agriculture and southern/western rice systems respond to different rainfall regimes.

**Simulation variables:** flood timing, irrigation maintenance, canal position, soil salinity, local rainfall, siltation, access to wells, labor mobilization, upstream/downstream conflict.

### Mediterranean dry farming
Many Aegean, Levantine, Anatolian, and European communities balanced cereals, pulses, vines, olives where appropriate, herding, woodland, and maritime resources. Annual rainfall variability could be more important than average temperature.

**Simulation variables:** winter rain, spring drought, harvest timing, crop mix, terrace/soil loss, herd mobility, storage, maritime access.

### Steppe and highland ecologies
Pastoral mobility is not synonymous with aimless nomadism. Herding strategies ranged from local grazing to seasonal transhumance to long-distance mobility. Kura-Araxes isotope work at Köhne Shahar, for example, found mostly local herding with limited transhumance and winter foddering, contradicting blanket reconstructions of long-range nomadism. [R14]

**Simulation variables:** pasture phenology, snow, water points, herd composition, seasonal camp rights, kin agreements, raiding risk, fodder reserves.

### Islands and coasts
Cyprus, Crete, the Cyclades, Sardinia, Britain/Ireland, and Gulf coasts make maritime skill, harbor conditions, timber, winds, and seaborne connectivity unusually consequential.

**Simulation variables:** sailing season, wind regime, ship capacity, harbor accessibility, pirates/raiders, crew trust, shipwreck probability, access to imported metal and prestige goods.

# Part II — The human-scale Bronze Age

## 3. The evidence hierarchy for simulated people

Use an evidence tag for every culture-specific behavioral rule:

- **A — Direct/contemporaneous:** letters, contracts, legal records, administrative texts, inscriptions, securely contextualized household assemblages, human remains, food residues, isotopes, or ancient DNA.
- **B — Strong synthesis:** convergent archaeology and specialist historical scholarship; mechanisms well supported but not individually observed.
- **C — Plausible reconstruction:** comparative anthropology plus local archaeological constraints.
- **D — Speculative:** serious scholarly hypothesis with material ambiguity.

Never turn D into a default behavior.

### What sources systematically miss

- Palace archives underrepresent informal exchange and illiterate villagers.
- Elite tombs overrepresent rank display.
- Destruction layers overrepresent crisis.
- Cemeteries are socially selected samples, not censuses.
- Ancient DNA measures biological relatedness, not automatically social kinship, ethnicity, language, or identity.
- Iconography shows idealized categories and ritual scenes, not a camera recording daily life.
- Later myths may preserve echoes of older institutions, but using them as direct Bronze Age testimony is hazardous.

## 4. Household, kinship, marriage, and inheritance

### Household as core unit
In many regions, the household combined:

- reproduction and child-rearing;
- food processing and storage;
- textile production;
- craft work;
- property and inheritance;
- care for elderly or disabled members;
- ritual obligations;
- debt and legal responsibility;
- political representation through a household head or senior members.

A “household” may include nuclear family, grandparents, siblings, married sons, servants, slaves, apprentices, dependents, fostered children, or attached laborers. Composition changes through death, marriage, divorce, migration, debt, and inheritance.

### Marriage as alliance and property arrangement
Romantic attachment certainly existed—love poetry and private correspondence make emotional attachment impossible to dismiss—but marriage often linked households and property. Depending on region and status it could involve bridewealth, dowry, gifts, negotiated residence, inheritance expectations, and diplomatic alliance.

**Old Babylonian example:** dowry normally traveled with a woman from her father's household; legal texts protected aspects of her dowry under divorce and inheritance, and some well-to-do women possessed seals, weights, and business property. [R15]

**Hittite example:** formal and de facto marriage were recognized; contracts and compensation regulated failed arrangements; prenuptial provisions could anticipate breakdown; divorce was not necessarily rare and could be initiated by women. [R16]

**Egyptian example:** Deir el-Medina documents show women owning/dividing property and engaging legal mechanisms. Naunakhte's famous will punished children who failed to care for her in old age, demonstrating that inheritance could encode reciprocal expectations rather than simple automatic descent. [R05]

**European examples:** genomic and isotopic work shows real variation. Leubingen's Early Bronze Age community was predominantly patrilineal/virilocal with female exogamy; Mokrin had female exogamy but no strict patrilocality and allowed women to inherit status; El Argar at La Almoloya appears strongly virilocal and patrilineal, with incoming women connecting groups and some evidence compatible with serial monogamy or polygyny. [R06][R07][R08]

### Simulation mechanics
Each household should carry:

`lineage_claims`, `residence_norm`, `marriage_market`, `dowry_or_bridewealth`, `inheritance_rule`, `care_obligation`, `adoption/fostering`, `divorce_cost`, `widowhood_rules`, `property_pool`, `dependents`, `household_reputation`.

Agents should negotiate marriage under **individual preference + household strategy + status + demography + political alliance + resource need**.

## 5. Childhood and the life course

Children are archaeologically underrepresented but socially indispensable. Mesopotamian evidence shows that infant mortality, socialization, adoption, labor, education, inheritance, and family continuity are recoverable only by combining texts and archaeology. [R17]

### Plausible life-course model

**Infancy:** high biological vulnerability; nursing and weaning timing vary. A household may use charms, prayers, protective figurines, midwives, wet nurses, or ritual specialists alongside practical care.

**Early childhood:** progressive participation in household work—animal tending, carrying water, food preparation, fetching fuel, sibling care, craft assistance—depending on ecology and status.

**Adolescence:** work specialization, apprenticeship, herd responsibility, textile labor, military preparation for some males, preparation for marriage, temple/palace service, or scribal schooling for a tiny minority.

**Adulthood:** marriage and household formation matter but are not universal; reproductive expectations and labor burden intensify. Elite heirs, priests, scribes, soldiers, merchants, dependent workers, slaves, and farmers inhabit sharply different risk structures.

**Old age:** seniority can increase ritual and household authority, but declining labor capacity can create dependence. Naunakhte's inheritance decision demonstrates that elder care could become a moral and economic ledger.

### Do not hard-code
A universal marriage age, life expectancy, childhood labor age, weaning age, or household size. These vary by region, class, sex, ecology, and period, and archaeological samples are biased.

## 6. Gender, sexuality, and social power

“Patriarchal” is often accurate at the structural level, but too coarse for simulation.

Separate:

- legal property rights;
- residence after marriage;
- inheritance;
- household decision power;
- work specialization;
- ritual office;
- political office;
- sexual norms;
- bodily autonomy;
- ability to litigate;
- mobility;
- social value of motherhood/fatherhood;
- elite versus non-elite opportunity.

A palace textile worker, a queen, an Ugaritic household manager, a Mycenaean priestess, an Egyptian property owner, a slave woman, and a mobile pastoralist woman cannot share one `female_role` template.

**Mycenaean texts** record thousands of women, many in palace-organized textile labor; some groups appear dependent or enslaved, while high-status religious women could control goods or land. Administrative texts differ between Knossos and Pylos, warning against assuming one Mycenaean labor regime. [R18]

**Ugarit** is commonly modeled as patriarchal and patrilocal, yet recent work on royal women emphasizes their active role in dynastic and political power. The simulation should allow structurally male-dominated institutions and consequential female agency simultaneously.

**Shang China** places royal/lineage authority strongly in kinship and ancestor ritual, but elite women such as Fu Hao demonstrate that at least some women could occupy exceptional military, ritual, and estate roles. Do not generalize exceptional elite status to the population, but do not prohibit it either.

## 7. Labor, occupation, and time

### Agriculture is seasonal, not continuous
A farming household's urgency changes by week: field preparation, sowing, irrigation, weeding, harvest, threshing, storage, pruning, lambing/calving, shearing, transhumance, fishing runs, or flood management.

### Common occupation families

- cereal farmer / gardener / orchard-vine worker;
- shepherd, goatherd, cattle herder, swineherd;
- fisher, hunter, gatherer, reed worker;
- potter, mason, carpenter, leather worker;
- spinner, weaver, dyer, fuller;
- miner, charcoal maker, smelter, smith, caster;
- shipwright, sailor, porter, caravan worker, donkey driver;
- merchant, broker, weighmaster;
- scribe, accountant, messenger, interpreter;
- priest, diviner, temple worker, healer;
- soldier, guard, chariot crew, archer, mercenary;
- palace/temple dependent, corvée laborer;
- domestic servant, slave, war captive;
- elite estate manager, official, ruler.

### Labor is often multiplex
People hold several roles. A farmer may perform corvée, raid seasonally, maintain canals, serve in a levy, brew beer, repair tools, and trade surplus. A Mycenaean named individual could have multiple administrative and social roles; rigid “class/job” assignment can be misleading. [R19]

### Simulation mechanic: annual labor calendar
Every settlement should have a calendar of **non-negotiable ecological windows**. Political demands that collide with harvest, lambing, or irrigation create resentment and real economic damage.

## 8. Food, drink, and the social meaning of consumption

No universal Bronze Age cuisine exists, but recurring foundations include cereals, pulses, domesticated animals, fermented beverages, fats/oils, dairy, fruits, gathered plants, and regionally important fish or game.

### Regional baselines

- **Mesopotamia:** barley especially important; dates in southern zones; beer; pulses; sheep/goat products; fish where available. Salinity favored barley over less salt-tolerant crops in affected irrigated zones.
- **Egypt/Nubia:** cereals, bread, beer, onions/vegetables, cattle/sheep/goats, fish, dates and other fruits; status affected meat access. Nubian cattle could carry strong economic and ritual meaning.
- **Anatolia/Aegean:** wheat/barley, pulses, sheep/goats/cattle/pigs; grapes and olives increasingly significant in many zones; honey/beekeeping where documented; marine foods locally.
- **Levant/Cyprus:** cereals, legumes, grapes/wine, olives/oil, caprines/cattle/pigs variably; fish and coastal resources; elite feasting and imported goods.
- **Indus:** wheat, barley, pulses, millets, sesame, dates and regional rice; cattle/water buffalo/sheep/goats; fish in river/coastal settings; cotton as a major fiber crop.
- **North China/Shang:** millet-based agriculture at the core, with wheat, barley, soybean, rice variably present; pigs/dogs/cattle/sheep and hunted animals. Fermented grain alcohol had ritual importance.
- **Sanxingdui/Chengdu Plain:** mixed rice and millet agriculture with pig/chicken and other livestock, supplemented by hunting, fishing, and gathering; archaeobotanical proportions remain debated. [R20]
- **Steppe/Central Asia:** animal products important, but “pastoralist = meat and milk only” is false. Archaeology shows fish, crops including millet, and exchange with farmers in some communities.
- **Bronze Age Britain:** the exceptional Must Farm assemblage preserves cereal foods, animal products, fish, wild resources, textiles, and complete household equipment, offering an unusually intimate domestic snapshot. [R21]

### Alcohol as a simulation variable
Beer, wine, and other fermented drinks can be calories, wages/rations, offerings, prestige commodities, feast media, diplomatic gifts, and intoxicants. Context matters more than a generic `drunkenness` rule.

### Feasting
Feasts can convert food surplus into alliance, hierarchy, ritual legitimacy, debt, and memory. Model host generosity and guest obligation, especially in chiefly and fortress-centered systems.

## 9. Health, disease, injury, disability

The simulation should model **risk environments**, not a single Bronze Age mortality table.

Common structural risks include:

- infectious disease intensified by settlement density, animals, water contamination, and mobility;
- dental wear/caries varying with diet;
- nutritional stress and seasonal shortage;
- maternal/infant risk;
- occupational trauma from mining, heavy labor, craft work, animals, and warfare;
- parasites and gastrointestinal disease;
- respiratory exposure from smoke, dust, furnaces, or indoor fires;
- violence ranging from household assault to raids and mass conflict.

Bioarchaeology can reconstruct diet, disease, trauma, activity, mobility, and demography but usually with cemetery-specific bias. [R22]

### Plague
Ancient DNA now demonstrates prehistoric *Yersinia pestis* lineages across Eurasia, including Early Bronze Age contexts. A 2026 study adds East Asian and Minusinsk Basin genomes and supports very early trans-Eurasian plague dispersal. This proves pathogen presence, not that every political collapse was a plague event. [R23]

### Harappan example
Bioarchaeological work at Harappa finds infectious disease and interpersonal violence unevenly distributed, especially in later and socially marginal samples. This contradicts the old image of an essentially violence-free Indus civilization, while still not justifying a “violent Indus” stereotype.

### Disability
Do not automatically remove disabled agents from economic life. Households can accommodate, reassign, support, exploit, or stigmatize depending on impairment, status, and social relations. Healed trauma and long survival after impairment can imply care.

## 10. Status, class, dependence, and inequality

Replace a single hierarchy ladder with overlapping status dimensions:

`birth_status`, `wealth`, `office`, `ritual_rank`, `military_rank`, `land_access`, `legal_status`, `palace_temple_attachment`, `lineage_prestige`, `gendered_rights`, `citizenship/community membership`, `foreignness`, `enslavement/debt`, `specialist_skill`.

### Wealth is not identical to authority
A wealthy merchant may lack aristocratic ritual rank; a priest may control sacred resources without large private land; a palace official may command labor but depend entirely on royal favor; a slave or dependent artisan may possess specialist leverage.

### Social mobility exists but is constrained
Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and other textual sources show promotion, patronage, marriage, military service, education, wealth accumulation, and office as pathways. Archaeogenetic studies in Europe suggest inherited status in some communities and more flexible achievement in others.

### Dependency continuum
Old Babylonian slavery scholarship emphasizes that debt could temporarily turn persons into dependents and that enslaved persons could sometimes own property or act in court; this is not evidence of humane slavery but of legal complexity. [R24]

**Simulation:** `freedom` should be multidimensional: mobility, labor choice, marriage choice, property, testimony, bodily vulnerability, redemption price, kin protection.

## 11. Law, justice, and dispute resolution

Bronze Age “law codes” are not modern statute books. Law can involve royal ideology, precedent, customary practice, written contracts, sworn testimony, family archives, elders, judges, temples, oracles, and negotiated compensation.

**Old Babylonian Mesopotamia:** written documentation and legal practice interacted through contracts, deeds, judges, scribes, oaths, and royal edicts. Property deeds could be transferred and preserved in family archives to defend later claims. [R25]

**Hittite law:** compensation often appears where modern readers expect punitive retaliation; law covers injury, property, sex, marriage, animals, slaves, and ritual concerns. Courts and local custom matter alongside written collections. [R26]

**Deir el-Medina:** surviving legal records overwhelmingly concern property and economic disputes because those were the matters households had reason to document. Absence of murder/rape records does not mean absence of violence; it reflects archival selection. [R27]

### Simulation dispute ladder
1. private negotiation;
2. kin mediation;
3. patron/elder intervention;
4. compensation/gift;
5. oath/divination/oracle;
6. formal court/official;
7. feud/retaliation;
8. flight or submission.

Which rung is available depends on society, status, and distance from state institutions.

## 12. Religion, ritual, divination, and causal belief

### Core principle
Religion should affect **decision-making under uncertainty**.

Possible ritual logics:

- gods/ancestors protect household or polity;
- ritual error or pollution causes misfortune;
- sacrifice establishes reciprocity with divine powers;
- divination asks which action is authorized or which threat exists;
- dreams and omens carry information;
- burial sustains ancestor relationships or rank memory;
- temple/royal ritual legitimizes hierarchy;
- household charms address birth, illness, animals, weather, and prosperity.

### Ugarit
Household religion is especially useful for simulation because texts and material evidence reveal concerns close to daily life: health, safe childbirth, livestock, weather, crops, prosperity, and family continuity. Ugarit's divine world is also organized through kinship language, making cosmic and household order conceptually resonant.

### Shang
Oracle-bone divination records royal questions about rain, harvests, war, hunts, sacrifice, childbirth, illness, and ancestral approval. Paired positive/negative divinations reveal formal management of uncertainty, but the corpus is a royal archive, not a transcript of commoner belief. [R28]

### Egypt
Oracles, dreams, household cults, state temples, ancestor reverence, magical-medical practice, and mortuary religion coexist. At Deir el-Medina, both men and women consulted the oracle of Amenhotep I, including over property disputes. [R29]

### Simulation belief architecture
Each agent should have:

- `cosmology_confidence`;
- `ritual_obligation_strength`;
- `preferred_specialist` (priest/diviner/healer/ancestor shrine/etc.);
- `omen_sensitivity`;
- `purity_taboo_set`;
- `ancestor_attachment`;
- `skepticism_about_specific_interpreters`;
- `willingness_to_pay_ritual_cost`.

This allows believers to disagree intensely about what the gods want.

## 13. Emotion, “personality,” honor, fear, and trust

### What can actually be supported
We cannot recover population-level Big Five scores for Hittites or Mycenaeans. We can recover culturally structured emotional situations.

**Old Babylonian letters** are full of anxiety, loneliness, disappointment, sympathy, and concern over reciprocal obligations. Elite-status men call one another brothers/friends/colleagues and police failures to behave accordingly. [R03]

**Deir el-Medina letters** preserve individual expression among family and friends, including women's voices, and can reveal affection, frustration, personality, and conflict. [R30]

**Amarna diplomacy** shows rulers obsessing over parity, gift quality, marriage, access, and whether another ruler has treated them as an equal “brother.” [R04]

**Shang divination** shows repeated anxiety management: will it rain, will childbirth be auspicious, will the campaign succeed, is an ancestor causing illness?

### Better simulation variables than “civilization personality”

- **trust radius:** household → lineage → village → patron network → stranger;
- **reciprocity sensitivity:** how strongly favors create obligations;
- **status sensitivity:** emotional response to insult, exclusion, rank loss;
- **risk tolerance:** affected by wealth, dependents, profession, recent shocks;
- **ritual security need:** desire for supernatural validation;
- **vengeance/compensation preference:** shaped by local dispute institutions;
- **mobility tolerance:** farmer, sailor, caravaner, herder, diplomat differ;
- **patron dependence:** willingness to defer for protection/resources;
- **future horizon:** storage, debt, inheritance, dynasty, immediate hunger;
- **out-group flexibility:** trade cosmopolitanism can coexist with strong local identity;
- **prestige orientation:** feasts, exotic goods, weapons, titles, piety, craft excellence can all be prestige currencies.

### Strong design warning
Never implement `Hittite = pragmatic`, `Mycenaean = warlike`, `Minoan = peaceful`, `Indus = egalitarian`, `Shang = cruel`, or `steppe = aggressive`. Those labels confuse selective evidence with a population psychology.

# Part III — Institutions and technologies that shape interaction

## 14. Bronze, mining, craft, and technological dependency

Bronze production has a chaîne opératoire:

1. locate/extract ore;
2. crush/beneficiate;
3. make charcoal/fuel;
4. smelt copper/tin or acquire metal;
5. transport ingots;
6. alloy;
7. cast;
8. hammer/finish/repair;
9. distribute;
10. recycle.

Each stage has specialists, environmental costs, and political value.

### Tin problem
Tin is rarer and its Bronze Age source networks remain actively researched. Uluburun isotope/provenance work has proposed Anatolian and Central Asian sources for much of the cargo, while newer analyses continue to debate western European contributions to Mediterranean tin. Do not hard-code one global mine-to-market route. [R01]

### Old Assyrian trade
Kültepe/Kaneš archives describe Assyrian merchant communities moving tin and textiles by donkey caravan into Anatolia and acquiring silver. Recent tin-isotope analysis supports imported high-tin material during the main trade period while complicating any single-source model. [R31]

### Recycling
Broken bronze is not useless. Metal is highly recyclable; hoards, remelting, and circulation can obscure provenance. Simulation should allow old weapons/vessels to become raw material during scarcity.

## 15. Writing, literacy, memory, and information speed

### Literacy is rare and institutionally concentrated
Cuneiform worlds had professional scribes; Egypt had scribal institutions; Mycenaean Linear B was heavily administrative; Shang writing survives primarily in elite divination; Indus signs remain undeciphered; much of Europe was non-literate.

**Mycenaean:** current scholarship treats Linear B literacy as narrowly distributed and primarily administrative. There is little evidence for a broad literate public; legal/social memory likely depended heavily on oral practice. [R32]

**Ugarit:** an exceptional multilingual scribal environment used alphabetic cuneiform alongside Akkadian and other scripts/languages, making it ideal for interpreter/scribe characters.

### Communication delays matter
A ruler may receive news days or weeks late. Messages can be lost, intercepted, mistranslated, exaggerated, or overtaken by events.

**Simulation:** every rumor/message gets `origin`, `transmission_chain`, `delay`, `distortion`, `authority`, `secrecy`, `language`, `literacy_requirement`.

## 16. Trade, markets, redistribution, and credit

Avoid the false binary “palace redistribution versus free market.” Ancient economies combine:

- household subsistence;
- rent/tax/tribute;
- palace and temple provisioning;
- rations;
- reciprocal gifting;
- merchant trade;
- market exchange;
- debt/credit;
- barter;
- weighed metal payments;
- labor obligations.

Coinage is not the default. Silver or other metals may be valued by weight. Standard weights and seals can support exchange without coins.

### Prestige goods versus bulk goods
Long-distance movement favors high value-to-weight items, but states can mobilize grain, timber, animals, and other bulk resources when routes permit. River and sea transport dramatically change cost.

### Prices are social as well as economic
Scarcity, status, political monopoly, ritual demand, credit relations, transport danger, and trust affect price. A merchant may accept worse terms from a reliable partner or patron.

---

## 17. Diplomacy and the “Great Kings” system

The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean/Near East had a recognizable interstate elite system connecting Egypt, Hatti, Babylonia, Mitanni, Assyria, and Alashiya/Cyprus, with smaller rulers nested in unequal relationships.

### Behavioral norms visible in Amarna letters

- Great Kings call peers “brother.”
- Gift exchange communicates parity and goodwill.
- Rulers complain when gifts are insufficient or low quality.
- Dynastic marriage is diplomacy.
- Vassal rulers appeal upward for troops, recognition, and intervention.
- Envoys can be delayed or effectively held.
- Personal relationship language masks hard bargaining.

### Simulation mechanic: diplomatic face
A king's utility function includes **material gain + parity + reputation among other rulers + dynastic security + credible reciprocity**. A materially small insult can matter if it publicly signals lower rank.

---

## 18. Warfare, raiding, fortification, and coercive reach

Bronze Age violence ranges from feud and cattle raiding to siege and imperial campaigning.

### Common forms

- local raid for livestock/people/resources;
- border skirmish;
- punitive expedition;
- levy army;
- elite chariot force;
- archery and infantry formations;
- siege/blockade;
- maritime raid/piracy;
- mercenary service;
- massacre/captive taking;
- symbolic destruction of palaces or cult places.

### State reach is uneven
Even large empires often govern through local rulers, tribute, hostages, marriage, garrisons, and periodic campaigning rather than continuous bureaucratic penetration of every village.

### Chariots
Spoked-wheel chariots appear in the Sintashta sphere around the early second millennium BCE and become prestige/military technologies across several regions. They require horses, specialist training, maintenance, open terrain, and support personnel. Their battlefield role is debated by time/place; do not treat them as universal Bronze Age tanks. [R33]

---

## 19. Migration, mobility, ethnicity, and identity

Ancient DNA proves major Bronze Age population movements in Eurasia, but material culture is not a genetic label and genes are not identity. [R34]

### Mobility types

- marriage migration;
- seasonal pastoral movement;
- merchant diaspora;
- military service;
- craft itinerancy;
- colonization/settlement;
- elite marriage/diplomatic relocation;
- captive deportation;
- refugee flight;
- imperial resettlement;
- gradual frontier mixing.

### Simulation identity stack
`household`, `lineage`, `village/city`, `region`, `language`, `cult`, `political allegiance`, `legal status`, `profession`, `ancestry stories`, `foreignness perception`.

Do not give every person a modern nation-like identity equal to an archaeological culture.

# Part IV — Regional civilization and culture profiles

The profiles below are written for simulation. “Character priors” are **not personality stereotypes**; they are pressures, opportunities, and default concerns that should bias decisions for a particular social position in a particular setting.

---

## 20. Southern Mesopotamia and Babylonia

### Geography and ecology
The lower Tigris-Euphrates alluvium is flat, hot, and agriculturally productive when water is controlled. Irrigation canals create both abundance and dependence on collective maintenance. River courses shift. Long-term irrigation can contribute to salinization, and barley's salt tolerance made it increasingly important in some zones. Southern cities rely on imported timber, stone, and metals because the alluvium lacks many raw materials.

### Major political phases
For simulation purposes:

- **Early Dynastic city-states:** competing urban polities such as Ur, Uruk, Lagash, Kish and others; temples, palaces, armies, irrigation, craft specialization.
- **Akkadian Empire:** first large territorial imperial formation in southern Mesopotamian history, integrating multilingual regions and campaigning far beyond the alluvium.
- **Ur III:** highly documented state administration, taxation/rations, provincial governance, large institutional labor systems.
- **Isin-Larsa / Old Babylonian:** political fragmentation, competing dynasties, Amorite royal houses, dense private archives, merchants, contracts and letters.
- **Kassite/Middle Babylonian:** Babylonia becomes a durable Late Bronze Age great power participating in international diplomacy.

Political labels should not overwrite city and household identity. A person in “Babylonia” may identify more intensely with city, temple, kin group, profession, or patron.

### Political economy
Palace and temple institutions own or manage substantial land and labor, but private households, local elites, merchants, craftsmen, and markets persist. Institutional redistribution does not replace exchange. Debt, rent, land tenure, silver by weight, barley obligations, and credit relationships are all plausible.

Local notables can mediate between central institutions and households. Ancient-state economic scholarship warns against imagining a perfectly centralized command economy. [R35]

### Household and family
Marriage contracts, dowries, adoption, inheritance, property transfer, and family archives are central. A household can preserve deeds over generations. Widows and priestly women may control significant property depending on status and context. Children are economic and lineage assets but also emotional family members.

### Social categories
Legal texts distinguish status categories, but lived status is more continuous. Free householders, palace/temple dependents, debtors, merchants, officials, soldiers, priests, enslaved persons, and foreigners overlap in complex ways.

### Religion and mental world
City gods, family gods, temple cult, omens, divination, dreams, incantations, healing and royal ideology coexist. Misfortune can be interpreted through divine displeasure, sorcery, oath violation, or ritual impurity alongside practical causes.

### Social psychology evidence
Old Babylonian private letters provide an exceptional foundation. Men of the awīlum/gentleman status group frame peers as “brothers,” “friends,” and colleagues and express distress when reciprocity fails. This suggests a simulation in which status exists partly through enacted relationships: failing to visit, help, answer, lend, advocate, or reciprocate can create real anxiety and threaten belonging. [R03]

### Typical agent concerns

**Small farmer/tenant:** water allocation; barley yield; rent/debt; draft animals; family labor; tax/corvée; marriage expenses; local patron.

**Merchant:** creditworthiness; seals/weights; caravan risk; partner reliability; silver value; legal documentation; access to powerful patrons.

**Scribe:** literacy prestige; institutional patron; accurate accounting; document custody; advancement; risk of blame.

**Urban notable:** reciprocal obligations; lawsuits; marriage alliance; land; office; status anxiety; relationship with ruler/temple.

**Dependent/slave:** work assignment; household treatment; family separation; redemption/manumission possibilities where applicable; patron protection.

### High-value simulation conflicts
- upstream canal diversion versus downstream fields;
- debt default after harvest failure;
- contested dowry or inheritance;
- merchant partner accused of withholding silver;
- temple/palace requisition colliding with household survival;
- ominous event interpreted as divine anger;
- ruler's troop levy during harvest.

### Major uncertainties
State archives are not census data; the countryside is less visible than cities; law collections do not equal uniform enforcement; emotional evidence is strongest among literate men and elite-connected families.

## 21. Northern Mesopotamia, Mari, and Old Assyrian merchant worlds

### Mari and middle Euphrates politics
Mari's archives illuminate a world of kings, governors, tribes, pastoral groups, queens, spies, messengers, soldiers, gifts, marriages, and constant information exchange. Political authority is relational: rulers negotiate with mobile groups and local elites rather than simply issuing commands to a passive territory.

Queen Shiptu and other palace women demonstrate that royal women can manage correspondence, ritual, household administration, and political affairs.

### Old Assyrian trade
Ashur's merchants created expatriate trading communities in Anatolian cities such as Kaneš/Kültepe. Merchants operated through family firms, partnerships, credit, written contracts, caravan organization, and local political arrangements.

Tin and textiles moved west; silver returned east. Donkeys, caravan personnel, tolls, weather, mountain passes, legal jurisdiction, and partner trust are simulation-critical. [R31]

### Character priors
A merchant can be highly mobile and cosmopolitan while remaining deeply bound to family obligations in Ashur. A caravan's decision logic should include seasonal route danger, tolls, local war, pack-animal health, debt maturity, and information about prices that may already be stale when received.

## 22. Hittite Anatolia

### Geography
The Hittite core sits on the central Anatolian plateau: cold winters, variable rainfall, uplands, valleys and passes. Agriculture combines cereals, legumes, cattle, sheep/goats, pigs in suitable areas, vines/orchards in some zones, and risk-buffering through mixed production. The empire stretches into ecologically and linguistically diverse territories.

### Political system
The Hittite kingdom/empire is dynastic and militarized but not an all-seeing bureaucracy. The king is warrior, judge, chief ritual actor, diplomat, and household head of the royal dynasty. Vassal treaties, oaths, local rulers, governors, hostages, marriage alliances, and campaigns bind peripheral regions.

Succession can be dangerous. Palace factions and royal kin matter. Elite politics should be modeled as a relationship network, not a clean constitutional hierarchy.

### Religion
“Hittite religion” is deliberately plural. Gods and cults from Hattian, Hurrian, Luwian, Mesopotamian, Syrian and other traditions enter the state pantheon. Ritual specialists diagnose impurity, oath violation, divine anger, and neglected festivals. The king's ritual calendar is politically consequential.

The famous royal plague prayers show a king reasoning historically and morally about epidemic disaster, inherited guilt, broken oaths, and divine punishment. That is evidence of an explanatory framework, not proof that disease was caused by the supposed offense.

### Law and household
Hittite legal texts are unusually useful for simulation: compensation, property, sex, injury, marriage, livestock, slaves and ritual offenses. Marriage could be formal or de facto; divorce and prenuptial arrangements existed, and women could initiate divorce. [R16][R26]

### Economy
Recent scholarship emphasizes mixed institutional control rather than total palace ownership. Elite authority can be financed through control of precious materials and politically mobilized goods while ordinary staples remain more locally managed. Festival and gift display turn economic resources into legitimacy.

### Warfare
Seasonal campaigning, fortified cities, chariots, infantry, allied/vassal contingents, scouts and siege are plausible. Logistics are constrained by distance and season. The empire's northern Kaska frontier demonstrates that “barbarian frontier” is an imperial viewpoint, not an anthropological description: Kaska communities had their own subsistence and settlement systems and alternated conflict, negotiation, and accommodation with Hatti.

### Character priors

**King/prince:** dynastic survival; oath legitimacy; military reputation; gods; rival kin; vassal reliability.

**Provincial governor:** collect obligations without provoking revolt; maintain roads/forts; report threats; manage local elites.

**Village household:** harvest; herd; tax/corvée; military levy; local cult; weather; distance from imperial protection.

**Ritual specialist:** correct diagnosis; procedural accuracy; patron trust; purity; rival interpretation.

### Do not encode
“Hittites were pragmatic and legalistic.” Their legal practice can look pragmatic to modern readers while their political and medical world was deeply ritualized.

## 23. Hurrian and Mitanni worlds

### Geography and society
Mitanni dominated parts of the Khabur region and northern Syria in the mid-second millennium BCE. Its subjects were linguistically and culturally diverse. Hurrian language and religion were influential well beyond the kingdom.

### Political identity
Mitanni is less textually transparent than Hatti or Egypt. A chariot-associated elite is prominent, and Indo-Aryan linguistic elements appear in elite technical/religious vocabulary, but this should not be turned into a simulation of a clean Indo-Aryan conquering caste without local evidence. Political structure and ethnic composition were more complex. [R36]

### Simulation use
Mitanni is excellent for agents with layered identities: Hurrian-speaking local, palace-connected chariot warrior, Syrian urban merchant, vassal ruler, foreign bride, or diplomat moving between Great Kings.

---

## 24. Western Anatolia, Arzawa/Luwian regions, and Troy

### Western Anatolian political landscape
The Hittite texts reveal multiple western polities rather than one coherent “Luwian civilization.” Luwian languages and hieroglyphic writing are important, but political identity is local and shifting.

### Troy
Late Bronze Age Troy occupied a strategic position near the Dardanelles, with a fortified citadel and lower settlement and connections across the Aegean, Anatolia and Black Sea approaches. Its exact political power and relationship to later Homeric tradition remain debated.

### Simulation
Model western Anatolia as a **frontier mosaic**: small kingdoms, fortified centers, traders, herders, Aegean connections, Hittite pressure, dynastic opportunism, and rapidly changing alliances.

---

## 25. Canaanite Levant

### Geography
The Levant compresses coastal plains, hills, river valleys, semi-arid zones, and major north-south/east-west routes. Rainfall gradients create strong local differences. Ports connect inland polities with Cyprus, Egypt and the Aegean.

### Middle Bronze Age
Fortified urban centers expand. “Canaanite” is a useful cultural-linguistic umbrella but not a unified state. City rulers compete and trade.

### Late Bronze Age under Egyptian hegemony
Many southern Levantine city rulers are Egyptian vassals. The Amarna letters show them complaining about rivals, requesting troops, accusing neighbors, and performing loyalty. Egyptian influence can coexist with strong local elite power.

### Social inequality
Late Bronze Age settlement patterns in some regions suggest powerful urban/estate elites and weaker rural settlement, but the scale and mechanism vary. Do not model every village as directly micromanaged by Egypt.

### Religion
Local temples, household cult, ancestor/family ritual, feasting, votive practice and regional deities coexist. Religious identities overlap with those at Ugarit and later Northwest Semitic traditions but should not be retrojected wholesale from later texts.

### Character priors
A local ruler may fear both neighbor and imperial governor. A farmer may experience “Egyptian empire” primarily as taxes, labor, elite extraction or occasional troops rather than daily Egyptian administration.

## 26. Ugarit

### Why Ugarit matters for simulation
Ugarit on the Syrian coast is one of the richest Late Bronze Age laboratories for ordinary and elite social systems because its archives preserve administration, diplomacy, literature, religion, economy, and multiple scripts/languages.

### Cosmopolitan scribal culture
Texts appear in alphabetic Ugaritic cuneiform and Akkadian, with Hurrian, Sumerian, Hittite, Egyptian, Luwian, and Cypro-Minoan contacts represented. A scribe or merchant in Ugarit can plausibly encounter people speaking several languages.

### Household structure
Urban archaeology indicates unequal house sizes and access to wells. Many wells were private, creating household-level infrastructure inequality. Patrilocal joint-family arrangements have been proposed for parts of the city, but not every household should be assumed identical.

### Household religion
The family provides a powerful metaphor for divine order. Everyday cult concerns include safe birth, health, prosperity, weather, crops and livestock. Local sanctuaries and communal drinking/feasting associations can create social networks beyond kin.

### Royal women
Newer scholarship challenges overly simple patrimonial models and shows royal women as active political actors.

### Economy
Ugarit sits at a maritime-commercial hinge connecting Syria, Anatolia, Cyprus, Mesopotamia and Egypt. Merchants, palace interests and international exchange overlap.

### Crisis
Ugarit was destroyed around the end of the Late Bronze Age, but famous “enemy ships” letters are only fragments of a complex regional breakdown. Simulation should model delayed intelligence, absent military forces, grain pressure, maritime insecurity and alliance failure without predetermining a single Sea Peoples cause.

## 27. Cyprus / Alashiya

### Geography and strategic resource
Cyprus is a major copper-producing island at the center of eastern Mediterranean shipping. Late Bronze Age settlement, ports, workshops and elite institutions become deeply entangled with copper production and exchange.

### Political organization
Texts refer to a king of Alashiya, often associated with Cyprus, but the degree of island-wide centralization remains debated. Archaeologists have proposed centralized and heterarchical models. The simulation should permit regional centers competing/cooperating under variable overarching authority.

### Merchants and specialists
Weights, seals, writing, copper workshops and imported luxuries suggest merchants and administrative specialists. At places such as Hala Sultan Tekke, copper and textile production intersect with extensive trade.

### Character priors
Copper elites want ore access, labor, charcoal/timber, furnaces, shipping and secure foreign demand. Sailors and merchants are exposed to storms, piracy, war and diplomatic embargoes. Local farmers may be integrated into industrial provisioning without personally trading abroad.

## 28. Middle Kingdom and New Kingdom Egypt

### Geography and state logic
Egypt is a long, narrow river civilization structured by the Nile valley and delta. The river allows unusually efficient transport relative to overland routes. Agricultural production follows inundation and local water management. Desert edges provide minerals and routes but also create strong settlement concentration.

### Kingship
The pharaoh is not merely a secular ruler; kingship participates in maintaining cosmic/social order. The concept of *maat*—order, rightness, proper arrangement—is central to elite ideology, but do not assume every peasant constantly verbalizes it.

### Middle Kingdom
Reunification after the First Intermediate Period produces a powerful central monarchy and literature preoccupied with order, loyalty, danger, administration and kingship. Forts and campaigns structure the Nubian frontier.

### New Kingdom imperial world
Egypt becomes an imperial power in Nubia and the Levant. War booty, tribute, foreign trade, gold from Nubia, and expanded temples enrich institutions. The pharaoh interacts with other Great Kings through diplomacy.

### Bureaucracy and local reality
Scribes, granaries, temples, estates, officials and work crews produce dense administration, but administration is uneven. Most people remain farmers or laborers whose lives are local.

### Deir el-Medina: exceptional human detail
The royal tomb-builders' village is not an average Egyptian village; it is a specialized, literate state-supported community. Precisely because of that, it preserves extraordinary evidence for human behavior.

Documents show:

- family/friend letters and distinctive personal voices;
- work attendance and wage/ration problems;
- private transactions;
- inheritance and wills;
- divorce and marital property;
- disputes and courts;
- oracles and dreams;
- scribal status;
- strikes/work stoppages;
- gossip, accusations, affection and resentment.

Naunakhte's will is especially simulation-friendly: she disinherited several children because they had failed to support her in old age, while rewarding those who did. This turns `care` into a reciprocal economic-moral obligation. [R05]

### Women
Women could hold and transfer property, bring disputes, inherit, divorce and conduct transactions, while political and occupational structures remained strongly gendered. Deir el-Medina women are unusually visible because of the site's documentation.

### Religion and medicine
Temple cult, household gods, ancestor cult, protective magic, dream interpretation, oracles and practical medical treatment coexist. Illness may prompt both material and ritual remedies.

### Character priors

**Farmer:** flood/harvest, rent/tax, family labor, local temple festivals, grain security.

**Tomb worker:** rations, crew politics, foreman/scribe, absenteeism, dangerous/technical work, family property.

**Scribe:** social authority through control of records and access to officials.

**Elite official:** royal favor, estate, temple networks, gifts, office succession.

**Soldier/mercenary:** campaign booty, land/reward, foreign service, patron.

## 29. Nubia: Kerma, C-Group, Medjay, and Egyptian imperial Nubia

### Geography
Nubia combines Nile corridors, cataracts, desert routes and pastoral zones. Control of river passage, gold regions, cattle and trade routes matters.

### Kerma
Kerma, centered in Upper Nubia, developed a complex polity rivaling Egyptian power in parts of the second millennium BCE. Monumental architecture, large cemeteries, craft production and strongly differentiated burials indicate hierarchy and centralized authority, but there is no indigenous written archive comparable to Egypt.

Cattle had economic, dietary, social and ritual importance; spectacular cattle-related funerary practices emphasize that livestock can be prestige and cosmology, not just calories.

### C-Group communities
Often associated with pastoral/agro-pastoral lifeways and distinctive burial traditions. Relations with Egypt range from trade and acculturation to conflict and mercenary service.

### Medjay
“Medjay” changes meaning over time—from groups associated with Nubian/desert regions to an Egyptian occupational designation for police/security in later periods. Do not make it a timeless ethnic tag.

### Egyptian conquest
New Kingdom Egypt incorporates much of Nubia through forts, temples, administrators, extraction and cultural transformation. Nubians can serve inside Egyptian institutions without losing all local identity.

### Simulation
Nubia is ideal for layered frontier identity: cattle herder, river trader, gold worker, Egyptianized official, local chief, mercenary, temple servant, cross-border family.

## 30. Minoan Crete

### Geography and economy
Crete combines fertile plains, mountains, caves, coastlines and maritime routes. Palatial centers such as Knossos, Phaistos, Malia and Zakros participated in storage, craft production and exchange. Timber, food, textiles, wine/oil and craft goods moved through local and international networks; metals and luxury raw materials were imported.

### Political organization
“Palace” does not automatically mean a centralized kingdom. Models range from hierarchical state-like systems to competing centers with complex local institutions. Archaeology increasingly resists a simple evolutionary story from egalitarian villages to unified palace rule.

### Writing
Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A remain undeciphered or only partly understood, sharply limiting direct access to language and administration.

### Religion
Peak sanctuaries, caves, springs, shrines, figurines, animal/human representations and votive body parts reveal ritual concerned with community, fertility, well-being and landscape. Elite and non-elite ritual overlap but differ in material investment.

### Food and household economy
Palaikastro and other archaeobotanical/zooarchaeological work shows mixed crop cultivation, herding, viticulture, olive management and marine use. Household wine-making is plausible in some settings. Recent site-level work reinforces local variation rather than one island-wide subsistence regime.

### Gender and imagery
Female figures are prominent in Minoan art, but **a Minoan matriarchy is not established**. Goddess-centered or matriarchal reconstructions remain interpretive hypotheses, not simulation defaults.

### Warfare
The old “peaceful Minoans” image is not sustainable as a blanket claim; weapons, fortification debates and conflict evidence exist, while the island also invested heavily in ritual and exchange. Do not force either pacifist or militarist identity.

### Character priors
A coastal merchant, palace craft specialist, upland herder, shrine participant and village farmer occupy different social worlds. Maritime opportunity should raise mobility and foreign-contact probability relative to many inland farmers.

---

## 31. Mycenaean Greece

### Political landscape
Late Bronze Age mainland Greece contains multiple palace-centered polities, not one nation-state. Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns, Thebes and other centers manage territories with varying administrative depth.

### Linear B administration
Tablets record land, livestock, offerings, personnel, military preparations, textiles, raw materials and obligations. Their accidental preservation—many baked in destruction fires—means they often represent short administrative snapshots.

### Labor and textiles
Textile production is a major palace concern. Large numbers of women are recorded in work groups, sometimes with children, and some categories are interpreted as dependent or enslaved. Pylos appears more centralized in textile management than Knossos, showing that “Mycenaean economy” must have local settings. [R18]

### Named persons and multiplex roles
Pylian records contain hundreds of personal names. Scholarship emphasizes that elites and commoners may hold multiple roles, undermining a rigid caste simulator. [R19]

### Religion
Linear B records deities, offerings and sanctuaries that include names later known in Greek religion, but Bronze Age practice is not identical to Classical Greek religion. Local sanctuaries, feasts, priestesses and palace offerings matter.

### Warfare
Fortifications, weapons, boar-tusk helmets, chariot records, warrior burials and administrative concern with coastal defense support substantial military capacity. That does not mean every free male is a professional warrior or that society's main cultural trait is aggression.

### Elite culture
Feasting, hunting, weapons, imported goods, monumental tombs and lineage display create status. Gift exchange and Aegean-wide elite styles can produce a shared elite culture across otherwise separate polities.

### Collapse/transformation
Around the 12th century BCE palace institutions and Linear B administration disappear across the mainland on differing schedules. Some centers are destroyed; some rebuild; Mycenaean material culture continues for generations. The correct simulation is **institutional failure with uneven community survival**, not instant population extinction. [R12]

### Character priors

**Palace official:** fulfill quotas; protect record accuracy; negotiate local elites; survive court politics.

**Textile worker:** ration security; family/work-group ties; supervisor; dependence status.

**Local landholder:** obligations to palace; lineage; feast participation; military levy.

**Warrior elite:** prestige competition; weapons/horses/chariot where relevant; patronage; inheritance.

**Sailor/merchant:** island/coastal network, piracy risk, multilingual contacts.

---

## 32. Cycladic and island Aegean communities

The Cyclades are not merely a bridge between Crete and mainland Greece. Island communities develop local ritual, burial, craft and exchange traditions. Seafaring knowledge, obsidian/stone traditions, later metals and regional brokerage make island social networks important.

**Simulation:** high maritime competence, small-community reputation, seasonal sailing constraints, and dependence on imported staples or raw materials where local ecology is limited.

## 33. Elam, Susa, Anshan, and the Iranian plateau

### Geography
Southwestern Iran links the Susiana plain, Zagros foothills/highlands and routes into Mesopotamia and the plateau. Other Iranian regions possess copper, stone and corridors toward Central/South Asia.

### Elam
“Elam” includes changing political configurations rather than one continuous centralized state. Susa is a major lowland center tied deeply to Mesopotamia; Anshan/highland regions contribute distinct political and cultural foundations.

### Economy and mobility
Irrigation agriculture, pastoralism, craft production and trade in metals, stone, timber and luxury materials overlap. Lapis lazuli and other eastern materials pass through Iranian networks.

### Identity
Elamite language and institutions coexist with Akkadian/Sumerian influence. A Susa scribe may operate in a cultural environment different from a highland pastoral chief while both participate in “Elamite” political worlds.

### Simulation
Iran should be a corridor and mosaic, not empty terrain between Mesopotamia and Indus/Central Asia.

## 34. Arabian Gulf: Magan, Dilmun, Umm an-Nar, Wadi Suq

### Magan / southeastern Arabia
Texts from Mesopotamia associate Magan with copper; archaeological evidence in Oman/United Arab Emirates shows substantial mining/metallurgy and maritime connections. Exact ancient territorial boundaries should remain flexible.

### Umm an-Nar communities
Third-millennium southeastern Arabia develops oasis agriculture, copper production, softstone industries, monumental collective tombs and long-distance trade. Sedentism and settlement increase, but “urban state” is not a safe universal description.

### Dilmun / Bahrain and Gulf networks
Dilmun becomes a major intermediary in maritime exchange among Mesopotamia, Iran, southeastern Arabia and the Indus world. Bahrain's burial landscapes and settlements reflect social differentiation and long-distance connectivity.

### Wadi Suq transition
After c. 2000 BCE, southeastern Arabia shows changes in settlement density, pastoral balance, tomb forms and exchange. This is better modeled as social-ecological reorganization than simple civilizational disappearance.

### Character priors
Oasis water rights, date/crop production, copper labor, camel **not yet as the later dominant long-distance caravan system**, donkeys/boats, seasonal sailing, pearl/fish resources, tribal/kin alliances and merchant brokerage matter.

## 35. Indus / Harappan civilization

### Geography and scale
The Indus tradition spans an enormous region including the Indus basin, Ghaggar-Hakra zones, Gujarat and connected areas. Mature urbanism (c. 2600–1900 BCE) includes Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira and many smaller settlements.

### Urban systems
Standardized bricks and weights, drainage/water infrastructure, craft specialization, seals, long-distance trade and planned urban spaces indicate powerful coordination mechanisms. But **we do not know that this was a centralized empire**.

### Political authority: major uncertainty
There are no securely identified royal tombs or giant ruler monuments comparable to Egypt/Mesopotamia. The famous “priest-king” label is speculation. Models include councils, corporate elites, merchant/landholding coalitions, ritual authorities, or more centralized systems whose monuments simply differ from Near Eastern patterns.

A simulation should expose `governance_model` as an uncertain scenario parameter rather than hide one speculative answer inside the code.

### Script
The Indus script remains undeciphered. Inscriptions are short, often associated with seals/tablets, and no accepted bilingual key exists. Therefore names, titles, theology, law and political offices are far less recoverable than in Mesopotamia. [R37]

### Economy
Mixed agriculture varies regionally: wheat/barley, pulses, millets, sesame, dates and rice in some zones. Cattle/water buffalo, sheep/goats and other animals matter. Cotton textiles are a major distinctive technology. Craft industries include beadmaking, shell, metallurgy, ceramics and weights/seals.

### Trade
Indus goods and references to Meluhha in Mesopotamian texts document Gulf/Mesopotamian connections, although mapping every term/person directly onto archaeological identity is difficult.

### Inequality and violence
Older popular accounts emphasized egalitarianism and peace because palatial and martial monuments are less obvious. Bioarchaeology complicates this. Harappa shows socially uneven disease exposure and trauma; interpersonal violence is present. Meanwhile, a 2026 Mohenjo-daro study argues that residential/infrastructural inequality may have been comparatively low in portions of the city. These findings are not contradictory if different forms of inequality existed at different sites/times. [R38]

### Religion
Without deciphered texts, reconstructions from figurines, seals, baths, fire features, animal imagery and later South Asian traditions are highly uncertain. Do not directly code later Hindu deities or caste institutions into Mature Harappan society.

### Decline/transformation
Urban contraction after c. 1900 BCE is gradual and regionally diverse. Shifting river systems, repeated drought episodes in some records, changing monsoon regimes, trade reorganization, crop adaptation and migration all likely matter. “Aryan invasion destroyed the Indus cities” is not an acceptable default model.

### Character priors

**Urban craft worker:** standardized measures, neighborhood networks, water access, specialist production.

**Merchant:** seals/weights, Gulf contacts, caravan/boat links, trusted partners.

**Farmer:** highly local crop/water ecology; monsoon uncertainty; herd/crop diversification.

**Administrator/elite (uncertain):** infrastructure coordination and standardization without assuming monarchic palace hierarchy.

---

## 36. BMAC / Oxus civilization

### Geography
The Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex centers on oasis settlements in modern Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Afghanistan and adjacent regions, c. 2300–1800 BCE in its classic urban phase.

### Economy
Irrigated/oasis agriculture supports fortified or monumental centers; craft production and long-distance exchange connect Iran, the Indus, Gulf and steppe. The surrounding environment makes water and irrigation management especially important.

### Political structure
Large sites such as Gonur and Togolok indicate hierarchy and organized construction, but exact government—kingdom, multiple chiefdoms, temple-centered systems, elite households—remains unclear. [R36]

### Mobile-sedentary interaction
BMAC communities interact with steppe pastoralists. Avoid a simple “civilized farmer versus nomad invader” model. Goods, people, marriages, livestock, technologies and ritual practices cross the boundary.

### Simulation
Strong scenario variables: irrigation reliability, oasis carrying capacity, caravan connections, fortress/elite extraction, pastoral relations, and migration pressure.

---

## 37. Sintashta, Andronovo, and Eurasian steppe Bronze Age

### Ecology
Grasslands and forest-steppe support mobile or semi-mobile pastoral economies, but settlements and agriculture occur too. Herds, river valleys, winter forage and seasonal movement structure life.

### Sintashta
Fortified settlements in the southern Urals, intensive metallurgy, elaborate burials, horse sacrifice and early spoked-wheel chariots make Sintashta distinctive. Chariot burials probably mark specialists/status, not ordinary transport for everyone. The scale and organization of metal production remain debated. [R33]

### Andronovo horizon
“Andronovo” covers a broad set of related archaeological traditions, not a unified polity. Mobility networks can persist over generations and link mining, herding, marriage and exchange.

### Food
Stable isotope and archaeobotanical evidence shows pastoral communities sometimes consumed fish and cultivated crops including millet. Do not model a pure meat/milk economy.

### Language
Links between Sintashta/Andronovo and Indo-Iranian language dispersals are influential hypotheses supported by converging linguistic, archaeological and genetic arguments, but a specific archaeological artifact cannot be assigned a spoken language with certainty.

### Social organization
Kurgan burials disproportionately show elites. Warrior/chariot imagery reveals prestige but cannot tell us the daily temperament of ordinary herders. Kin, herd ownership, seasonal camps, reciprocal pasture access and marriage networks are safer social variables.

### Character priors
Herd security, horse quality, kin alliance, pasture, metal access, mobility, hospitality, raiding risk, marriage network and ritual feasts/sacrifices.

---

## 38. Seima-Turbino and long-range technological networks

Seima-Turbino describes a striking horizon of metal objects distributed across a vast Eurasian range in the early second millennium BCE. New metallurgical studies support rapid movement of tin-bronze technologies and objects, potentially involving mobile craftspeople and social networks rather than a territorial empire.

**Simulation use:** a traveling metalworker network can transmit technology faster than mass population migration.

---

## 39. Tarim Basin and Inner Asian corridors

Ancient DNA from Early Bronze Age Tarim mummies demonstrates that striking material connections do not necessarily equal recent migrant ancestry. The Tarim individuals sampled can have local ancestry while adopting crops, livestock, clothing technologies or ideas moving through wider networks. [R39]

**Simulation lesson:** technologies and cultural packages can diffuse through contact without population replacement.

---

## 40. Erlitou and the emergence of Bronze Age states in China

### Chronology and identity
Erlitou (roughly c. 1900–1500 BCE) is a major early state-level center in the Central Plains. Some scholarship links it to the historically remembered Xia dynasty, but the identification is contested. Simulation data should call the archaeological culture/site Erlitou unless a scenario explicitly chooses a Xia hypothesis.

### Political economy
Palatial compounds, roads, elite burials, bronze workshops and craft specialization indicate strong hierarchy and resource mobilization. Early bronze ritual forms develop in an institutional context.

### Regional diversity
Erlitou is not the sole center of early Chinese complexity. Contemporary and preceding regional centers demonstrate multiple trajectories.

---

## 41. Shang China

### Geography and political form
Shang political power centers on the Central Plains, with capitals shifting over time; Late Shang Anyang/Yinxu supplies the richest written and archaeological evidence. Royal power operates through kinship, allied lineages, military campaigns, ritual authority, tribute/exchange and mobile personal kingship rather than a modern territorial bureaucracy.

### Economy
A 2025 Cambridge synthesis emphasizes domestic and institutional economies, specialization, distribution and commercialization while warning that evidence remains incomplete. Millet agriculture is foundational in the north; animals, craft workshops, bronze production, bone working and long-distance raw-material procurement support the capital. [R40]

### Royal divination
Oracle bones are a unique behavioral dataset. Royal questions concern:

- rain and harvest;
- war and raids;
- hunting;
- sacrifice;
- childbirth;
- illness/toothache;
- ancestral approval;
- timing of action.

They reveal elite anxieties and decision processes but are not a diary of the general population. [R28]

### Ancestors and kinship
Royal ancestors are political actors in the Shang worldview. Sacrifice helps sustain and negotiate relationships among living kin, deceased ancestors and divine powers. Lineage membership, burial location and access to ritual resources structure status.

### Violence and sacrifice
Archaeology and texts document warfare, captive taking and large-scale human sacrifice. Isotope studies show some sacrificial victims were non-local and may have lived in the region for years before death. This indicates institutionalized coercive violence but does not imply indiscriminate everyday brutality among all residents.

### Political reach
Campbell's model emphasizes networks, boundaries and personal royal mobilization. Campaigns, hunting circuits, gifts, allied lineages and sacrifice help produce sovereignty. Peripheral groups can rebel, ally, raid or be incorporated. [R41]

### Character priors

**Royal lineage member:** ancestor obligations, succession, sacrifice, war, gift distribution.

**Lineage elite:** loyalty and rivalry; access to bronze ritual; local labor mobilization.

**Craft specialist:** workshop attachment; raw-material supply; production quota; patronage.

**Farmer:** millet crop, local kin, labor extraction, animal raising; royal politics may be distant until levy/tribute arrives.

**Captive/dependent:** origin identity, forced relocation, household/institutional attachment, escape/social integration possibilities.

---

## 42. Sanxingdui and Bronze Age Sichuan / ancient Shu traditions

### Why it matters
Sanxingdui demonstrates that Bronze Age China was not culturally monolithic. On the Chengdu Plain, a distinctive walled center and ritual tradition produced monumental bronzes, masks, gold, jade and ivory unlike the Central Plains canon.

### Ritual deposits
New excavations of pits K3–K8 produced more than 17,000 objects and materials including bronze, jade, gold, ivory and silk remnants. A 2025 analysis dates the pits broadly to the Late Shang period and interprets them as ritual/sacrificial deposits, while a 2026 geoarchaeological study of Pit K4 refines the depositional sequence and cautions that fire residues were introduced from external burning rather than created in situ. [R42][R43]

### Subsistence
A 2024 synthesis argues for mixed rice and millet agriculture with pig/chicken and other livestock, plus continued hunting/fishing/gathering; exact crop balance remains debated because the archaeobotanical record is incomplete. [R20]

### Political structure
The relation between Sanxingdui archaeological culture and later traditions of the Shu kingdom is plausible but historically reconstructed. We do not possess a contemporary local written archive comparable to Shang oracle bones.

### Simulation
Do not make Sanxingdui a mysterious “lost alien-like civilization.” Model a wealthy regional center with distinctive ritual specialists, metalworkers, ivory procurement, farmers in a fertile plain, elite competition, and cultural exchange with neighboring regions.

---

## 43. Kura-Araxes and the South Caucasus

### Kura-Araxes
Early Bronze Age communities across the South Caucasus and adjacent highlands share ceramic and domestic traditions while remaining regionally diverse. Archaeologists often reconstruct extended-household agro-pastoral societies with less pronounced hierarchy than the succeeding kurgan traditions.

Recent settlement and isotope studies caution against the old assumption that Kura-Araxes expansion was driven by long-distance nomadic pastoralists. At Köhne Shahar, herds were mostly locally managed with limited transhumance and winter fodder. [R14]

### Early Kurgan / Trialeti transformations
Later rich kurgans with precious metals and wheeled-vehicle symbolism indicate stronger rank differentiation and different political values. The transition is not simply “civilization arrives”; it is a reorganization of mobility, prestige and hierarchy. [R44]

### Late Bronze Age fortress polities
In Armenia, fortress-centered political systems among mobile pastoralists challenge the claim that mobility prevents complex governance. Feasting, ritual and elite production can anchor pastoral groups to institutions even without dense urbanism. [R45]

### Simulation
Caucasus scenarios should allow household farming/herding, short-range transhumance, mining routes, fortress ritual, kurgan prestige, metal exchange and political complexity without cities.

---

## 44. Central Europe: Únětice and related Early Bronze Age communities

### Social hierarchy
Únětice contexts show surplus farming, metal exchange and spectacular elite burials alongside ordinary cemeteries. Leubingen genome/isotope work reconstructed five pedigrees among 46 individuals and found predominantly patrilineal/virilocal organization with female exogamy. Grave goods varied with sex, age and locality. [R06]

### Elite versus household society
“Princely” graves do not prove a state. Local chiefs may control trade, metal, land, ritual or feasting while ordinary households remain economically substantial.

### Simulation
Model local chiefdom politics with prestige goods and marriage networks rather than palace bureaucracy.

---

## 45. Mokrin / Maros communities of southeastern Europe

Mokrin provides a valuable counterexample to overly uniform European kinship reconstruction. Ancient DNA finds female exogamy but no strict patrilocality; status was not separated by rigid marriage barriers, women could inherit status, and sons could potentially achieve status during life. [R07]

**Simulation lesson:** neighboring Bronze Age communities can share material traditions while running different inheritance and residence systems.

---

## 46. El Argar, southeastern Iberia

### Settlement and inequality
El Argar (from roughly 2200 BCE) shows fortified hilltop settlements, specialized production, marked dietary and burial inequality, metal weapons and strong political centralization relative to many European contemporaries.

### Kinship
La Almoloya ancient DNA indicates virilocal, patrilineal organization and reciprocal female exogamy, with paternal pedigrees extending several generations. Incoming women likely connected political groups. Some paternal half-siblings are consistent with serial monogamy or polygyny. [R08]

### Simulation
Household marriage is a political network. Hereditary rights, grain/food access, weapons and elite control can produce strong class conflict. Do not assume every Iberian Bronze Age society resembles El Argar.

---

## 47. Bronze Age Britain and Ireland

### Metal and mobility
Copper mining, including major operations such as Great Orme, and later tin supply from Cornwall/Devon integrate Britain into wider metal systems. How much British tin reached the Mediterranean and when remains actively debated.

### Settlement and households
Much of the population lives in farmsteads or small settlements rather than cities. Roundhouses, field systems, cattle/sheep, cereals, weaving and local exchange structure everyday life.

### Must Farm
The c. 850 BCE Must Farm pile-dwelling settlement is later than the eastern Mediterranean Bronze Age but an extraordinary household time capsule. Its rapid destruction preserved wooden architecture, textiles, tools, pottery with food remains, glass beads and domestic assemblages. It demonstrates the richness of “ordinary” prehistoric material life that usually disappears archaeologically. [R21]

### Warfare
Weapons and the Tollense battlefield in northern Europe show organized lethal conflict, but they should not convert all Late Bronze Age households into warrior bands.

---

## 48. Nordic Bronze Age

### Economy and connectivity
Southern Scandinavia lacks local copper/tin and therefore depends on long-distance metal exchange. Cattle, farming, maritime mobility, amber and prestige exchange underpin social hierarchy.

### Ritual and imagery
Rock art, bronze deposition, monumental burials, ships, sun imagery, weapons and processions are central evidence, but interpretations remain contested.

### Warrior identities
Elite warrior display exists, yet warfare intensity varies by region/time. Maritime and ritual prestige can be as important as combat.

### Simulation
High dependence on exchange means foreign metal shortages can transform prestige systems without causing starvation. Amber and livestock can become export leverage.

---

## 49. Carpathian Basin fortified networks

The Carpathian Basin hosted dense fortified settlements and complex hierarchical networks in parts of the second millennium BCE. Recent survey work complicates old narratives of simple abandonment after c. 1600 BCE, showing reorganization and shifting centers rather than clean regional collapse.

**Simulation:** settlement aggregation, fortification labor, metal routes, farming surplus and alliance networks can rise and disintegrate without a single empire.

---

## 50. Terramare, northern Italy

### Settlement system
Terramare communities of the Po plain (roughly 17th–12th centuries BCE) built dense, planned settlements with ditches, embankments and substantial infrastructure. Population growth and intensive agro-pastoral production supported an unusually concentrated settlement network.

### Collapse
Older models proposed combined demographic pressure, deforestation/land degradation and aridity. A recent University of Bologna project finds evidence for early improved nutrition and labor-demanding infrastructure, later reduced mobility, warming/drying around 1200 BCE and possible food-production crisis, while failing to find pathogen DNA sufficient to explain abandonment. [R46]

### Simulation
Terramare is a valuable endogenous-collapse scenario: population and infrastructure create prosperity, then maintenance and food requirements make the system sensitive to rainfall, land pressure and labor shortage.

---

## 51. Nuragic Sardinia

### Landscape
Thousands of nuraghi—stone towers/complexes—structure Bronze Age Sardinian landscapes alongside villages, tombs and sanctuaries. Exact functions vary: residence, defense, territorial display, ritual and community coordination are all proposed in different cases.

### Society
Shared architecture and material practice coexist with local communities. Agriculture, herding, craft, metallurgy and Mediterranean contact intensify through time.

### Simulation
Use decentralized tower-centered communities with local rivalries, ritual gatherings, pastoral/agricultural resources and growing foreign merchant contact; do not assume island-wide political unity.

---

## 52. Urnfield transformations in Europe

Cremation and urn burial spread widely in later Bronze Age Europe, but this is not evidence for one invading “Urnfield people.” Regional communities adopt, adapt and reject funerary practices differently.

**Simulation lesson:** cultural style can spread through emulation, marriage, ritual prestige and mobility without total population replacement.

---

## 53. Bronze Age Southeast Asia: a later chronology

Mainland Southeast Asia's Bronze Age begins much later than Mesopotamia's, roughly in the late second millennium BCE depending on chronology/site. Copper-base metallurgy enters existing farming societies and contributes to new exchange networks and social differentiation, but it does not instantly generate Near Eastern-style states.

Recent archaeometallurgical work emphasizes complex exchange of metals and technical knowledge. Treat this as a distinct simulation era rather than an extension of the 2000 BCE Near East.

---

# Part V — Cross-cultural everyday systems

## 54. Settlement types and what living there feels like

### Palace/temple capital
High population, specialist quarters, officials, storage, foreign visitors, inequality, surveillance, ritual calendar, disease risk, labor demand.

### Secondary town
Local market, craft production, governor/elite residence, defensive wall, mixed farmers and specialists; more direct relation between residents and local authorities.

### Farming village
Kin density, seasonal labor, livestock smell/noise, shared wells or canals, intense reputation economy, limited literacy, periodic state demands.

### Pastoral camp
Mobility, herd-centered schedule, weather exposure, water negotiation, camp kinship, trading visits, raiding risk, flexible architecture.

### Mining/smelting community
Specialist skill, fuel demand, toxic/smoky environment, transport dependence, political interest, boom/bust cycles.

### Port
Foreigners, interpreters, ships, storage, customs/tolls, sailors, sex/work markets, piracy intelligence, imported foods/styles, epidemic exposure.

### Fortress
Concentrated warriors/officials, storehouses, dependent villages, signaling, refuge during raids, ritual/political gatherings.

---

## 55. Material culture that should surround characters

A believable Bronze Age simulation world is filled with perishable and mundane objects, not only bronze swords:

- clay cooking/storage/serving vessels;
- grinding stones and querns;
- baskets and mats;
- wooden bowls, tools and furniture;
- textile looms, spindle whorls, needles;
- wool/linen/cotton regionally;
- leather, hides, rope, nets;
- reed products;
- sickles, hoes, plows;
- weights, seals, tokens/tablets in administrative zones;
- storage jars, silos, granaries;
- lamps and hearths;
- manure, ash, food waste;
- animal pens;
- amulets and household ritual objects;
- repaired/reused objects.

Bronze may be prestigious and strategically important while stone, wood, bone and ceramic remain numerically dominant in daily life.

---

## 56. Clothing and appearance

Clothing is region-, class- and climate-specific. Wool is central across much of western Eurasia; linen is important in Egypt; cotton is important in the Indus world; leather/fur varies with climate. Dyes, embroidery, patterned textiles, jewelry, hairstyles and headgear can signal rank, profession, gender, age, region or ritual status.

Textiles are labor intensive and economically major. A simulation should track fleece/flax/cotton → preparation → spinning → weaving → dyeing/finishing, not spawn clothing as free household output.

---

## 57. Hygiene, water and waste

Avoid both extremes “ancients were filthy” and “advanced drainage = modern sanitation.”

Water access depends on river, well, cistern, canal and household infrastructure. Bathing and purity practices can be culturally important. Urban drains can manage water without eliminating contamination. Animals, refuse, smoke, mud, dung fuel, insects and standing water are ordinary environmental features.

Private wells at Ugarit and elaborate water systems at Indus cities show that infrastructure can be socially uneven even in sophisticated cities.

---

## 58. Transportation

### Human portage
Still fundamental, especially inside settlements and over short distances.

### Pack animals
Donkeys are essential to many Near Eastern caravan systems. Cattle pull carts/plows. Horses gain major prestige and military importance; riding exists but scale and military use vary.

### Wheeled vehicles
Heavy carts/wagons move goods where terrain/routes allow. Spoked-wheel chariots are specialist prestige/military technology.

### River transport
Nile, Euphrates/Tigris and other waterways massively reduce bulk transport costs.

### Sea
Sailing ships and oared vessels connect islands/coasts. Voyages are seasonal and weather-sensitive. Navigation can use coastline, stars, winds and accumulated pilot knowledge without modern maps.

---

## 59. News, rumor and information ecology

Information should be a strategic resource.

Sources:

- returning merchant;
- messenger/envoy;
- soldier;
- migrant/refugee;
- traveling craft specialist;
- marriage connection;
- temple oracle/diviner;
- market gossip;
- captured enemy;
- official letter.

Rumor accuracy should decline with hops and increase with independent confirmation. Powerful actors may manipulate information deliberately.

---

# Part VI — Environmental, social, political, and systemic problems

## 60. The core problem: risk is local, cascading failure is networked

The most useful model of Bronze Age instability is a layered one:

**environmental shock → household loss → price/debt stress → institutional extraction or relief → migration/conflict decisions → trade disruption → political legitimacy effects → second-order shortages.**

No link is automatic. A drought can be absorbed by storage or trade; the same drought can become famine if elites continue extraction, transport fails, or warfare blocks movement.

---

## 61. Drought and rainfall variability

### 4.2 ka event: important correction
For decades, scholarship often invoked a widespread c. 2200 BCE drought to explain the Akkadian transformation, Old Kingdom difficulties, changes in the Levant, Indus developments and other events. Regional aridity is genuinely documented in some archives. But a 2024 Nature Communications synthesis of 1,142 Holocene paleoclimate datasets concluded that the “4.2 ka event” is not a globally coherent or especially remarkable climate excursion; records around 4.2 ka include dry, wet and null signals, and publication has suffered from confirmation bias. [R09]

### Simulation implication
Never schedule a global `4200BP_megadrought`. Generate **regional hydroclimate anomalies** with independent intensity/duration. Then expose communities to them through local ecology.

### Regional examples

- Parts of Mesopotamia/northern Syria may experience multi-year drought and abandonment/reorganization while neighboring zones remain viable.
- South Asian records support episodes of monsoon weakening and repeated drought relevant to Harappan adaptation, but urban decline is long and spatially variable.
- A 2025 study in the Huai River region found wetter rather than drier conditions around the supposed 4.2 ka event, demonstrating regional inversion.

---

## 62. Irrigation, salinization, and water politics

### Mesopotamian salinity
Irrigation in hot, low-gradient landscapes can raise salts when drainage is poor. Archaeobotanical and historical patterns are consistent with barley's expansion partly because it tolerates salinity better than wheat.

### Simulation dynamics

`irrigation_intensity ↑` can increase near-term yield but also:

- require canal labor;
- create headwater/tail-end inequality;
- increase salinity under poor drainage;
- create political control points;
- spread waterborne disease;
- become vulnerable to warfare or neglect.

### Water legitimacy
A ruler or local official who fails to maintain canals/wells can lose legitimacy even if the climate is not unusually bad.

---

## 63. Floods, river shifts, and harbor change

Floods can destroy crops, houses, stored food and canals while depositing fertile silt. River avulsion can isolate a city from transport or irrigation. Delta/coastal sedimentation can alter ports.

**Simulation:** geographic infrastructure should age. A successful port in 1500 BCE is not guaranteed to remain equally accessible in 1200 BCE.

---

## 64. Deforestation, fuel, soil pressure, and metallurgy

Agriculture, construction, ships, domestic fuel and especially charcoal for metallurgy consume wood. Local deforestation can increase travel distance for fuel, erosion and political competition over wooded zones.

Do not assume metal industries always caused regional ecological collapse; quantify production scale and local forest regeneration. But make fuel a real input rather than an invisible resource.

Terramare collapse models are especially useful for exploring interacting population growth, land pressure and increasing aridity. [R46]

---

## 65. Pasture failure and herd disease

Pastoral wealth can disappear quickly through drought, snow/ice events, epidemic livestock disease, raiding or blocked migration routes.

A herder under pasture stress may:

- move earlier;
- request access from kin;
- sell animals at depressed prices;
- raid;
- enter wage/mercenary service;
- migrate into farming zones;
- slaughter breeding stock, harming future recovery.

This is a classic nonlinear household trap.

---

## 66. Crop failure and storage

### Household storage
Small stores buffer ordinary variability but can be consumed by seed needs, pests, mold, theft or taxes.

### Institutional granaries
Large institutions can smooth local shocks if they redistribute; they can intensify famine if they extract and concentrate grain for armies, temples, elites or exports.

### Seed constraint
After a bad year, eating seed grain improves survival today but reduces planting capacity next year. This should be an explicit desperate decision.

---

## 67. Disease and epidemic uncertainty

### What is known
Prehistoric plague existed. Tuberculosis, leprosy and other infections appear in ancient populations. Dense settlements and long-distance mobility can spread pathogens.

### What is not known
There is no evidence for a single giant epidemic explaining all Bronze Age collapses. Even at Akhetaten/Amarna, a recent osteological reassessment finds insufficient evidence for the once-popular claim of a catastrophic epidemic despite contemporary Hittite plague texts elsewhere. [R47]

### Simulation
Diseases should have:

`pathogen`, `transmission`, `incubation`, `mortality/severity distribution`, `seasonality`, `immunity`, `mobility network`, `nutrition interaction`, `social response`.

Ritual responses—prayers, purification, scapegoating, quarantine-like avoidance, pilgrimage, intensified sacrifice—may amplify or mitigate spread depending on behavior.

---

## 68. Earthquakes and natural hazards

The eastern Mediterranean is tectonically active. Earthquakes can damage walls, palaces, cisterns and ports and produce fires. “Earthquake storm” explanations for the 1200 BCE transition remain debated because destruction layers are hard to diagnose and chronology may not synchronize.

**Simulation:** earthquakes are credible local/regional shocks, not a universal collapse trigger.

---

## 69. Debt spirals and household failure

A bad harvest can create:

1. grain loan;
2. pledge of field, house, child labor or person depending on legal system;
3. next harvest partly owed to creditor;
4. inability to recover herd/seed;
5. deeper dependence.

Royal debt remissions in Mesopotamia make sense in a world where excessive debt could destabilize the tax/levy base and concentrate dependent labor.

### Character psychology
Debt creates shame, patron dependence, resentment, family conflict and flight incentives. Wealthy creditors must balance extraction against destroying clients.

---

## 70. Inequality and elite overreach

High inequality is not automatically unstable. Instability grows when elites lose the ability to convert extraction into protection, ritual legitimacy, food security, successful warfare or prestige distribution.

Possible feedback:

`elite competition → higher display/war costs → more extraction → household stress → evasion/migration/revolt → lower revenue → harsher extraction.`

This is a useful hypothesis for some palace systems but should be scenario-tested, not declared the cause of every collapse.

---

## 71. Succession crises and dynastic politics

Monarchies can be especially vulnerable at succession:

- multiple eligible princes;
- child ruler;
- queen/queen mother factions;
- rival branches;
- foreign marriage claims;
- military commanders;
- vassals testing weakness;
- divine legitimacy disputes.

A single ruler death can therefore change alliance networks far beyond the palace.

---

## 72. Corvée, taxation, and extraction limits

States require labor for:

- canals;
- walls;
- palaces/temples;
- roads;
- army service;
- mining;
- harvest for estates;
- transport.

Extraction has an opportunity cost. Pulling farmers into a campaign during agricultural bottlenecks can reduce food supply. The simulation should let rulers accidentally damage their own fiscal base.

---

## 73. Warfare as economic shock

War effects extend beyond casualties:

- fields abandoned;
- orchards cut;
- herds seized;
- irrigation damaged;
- craftsmen captured;
- trade rerouted;
- refugees enter neighboring communities;
- taxes rise;
- elite gifts/tribute disappear;
- legitimacy shifts.

The conquered population may not be exterminated; institutions and allegiance can change while households persist.

---

## 74. Raiding, piracy, mercenaries, and ambiguous violence

The same armed group can be described differently by different actors: mercenary to one ruler, pirate to a merchant, refugee-warband to itself, “enemy people” in royal propaganda.

This ambiguity is critical for the so-called Sea Peoples. Egyptian texts and images identify named groups and battles, but modern “Sea Peoples” is a collective scholarly label, and debate continues over whether particular groups were primarily migrants, raiders, mercenaries, coalitions or combinations. A 2022 study of Egyptian terminology argues some groups look more like relatively small raiding/warrior bands than mass migrant populations, while other scholarship points to family groups and migration. [R48]

### Simulation
Create armed groups from **people with histories**, not spawned invasion units:

- disbanded soldiers;
- displaced households;
- maritime traders turned raiders;
- mercenary networks;
- frontier migrants;
- defeated elites;
- opportunistic pirates.

---

## 75. Migration and refugee pressure

Migration is a strategy, not a cause category.

Push/pull variables:

- crop/herd failure;
- war;
- marriage;
- labor opportunity;
- land shortage;
- trade opportunity;
- political invitation;
- deportation/capture;
- religious/political refuge.

Hosts decide whether newcomers are kin, clients, workers, soldiers, marriage partners, competitors or threats.

---

## 76. Trade network fragility

A highly connected system is both resilient and vulnerable.

### Resilience
If one supplier fails, trade can reroute; allies can send grain; merchants carry information; specialized production finds wider markets.

### Vulnerability
If several critical nodes fail together, specialization becomes a liability. Tin, copper, timber, grain or shipping shortages cascade. A 2024 network-science model of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean finds many single-node failures absorbable but some combinations capable of cascading network breakdown. The model is a mechanism demonstration, not proof of the exact historical sequence. [R10]

### Simulation
Calculate network centrality dynamically. A port's destruction matters according to what alternatives exist that year.

---

# Part VII — The Late Bronze Age collapse in simulation detail

## 77. What actually changed around 1200 BCE

Across parts of the Aegean, Anatolia, Cyprus, Levant and eastern Mediterranean between the later 13th and 12th centuries BCE:

- Mycenaean palace administrations disappear;
- Hittite imperial government collapses;
- Ugarit is destroyed and not rebuilt as the same kingdom;
- international diplomatic networks contract;
- some cities are destroyed or abandoned;
- population shifts occur;
- new settlement patterns and material traditions appear;
- writing disappears in some regions but persists elsewhere;
- iron becomes increasingly important later, but the crisis is not simply “iron beats bronze.”

Egypt and Babylonia do not vanish. Assyria survives. Cyprus and the Levant exhibit major continuity as well as disruption. Local trajectories vary. [R11][R12]

---

## 78. Candidate causes and evidence strength

### Climate stress — B/C depending locality
Some paleoclimate proxies support drying in parts of the eastern Mediterranean; others do not, and high-resolution work shows substantial spatial heterogeneity. Climate can be a force multiplier but does not explain destruction timing by itself.

### Famine/food shortage — B locally
Texts from the Hittite/Ugaritic world and Egyptian relief/text traditions include food stress contexts, but translating them into a single regional famine is difficult.

### Warfare/internal conflict — A/B
Destruction, fortification, weapons, royal records and political fragmentation support conflict. Identifying attacker identity is often much harder.

### Migration — A/B as phenomenon, C/D for specific group narratives
Mobility is clear in archaeology/genetics/isotopes; attributing particular destructions to named migratory “peoples” is much more contested. Knapp's *Migration Myths* is a major caution against simplistic ethnic migration narratives. [R49]

### Sea Peoples — A that named enemy groups appear in Egyptian records; C/D for unified-cause model
Treat as several interacting groups/episodes, not one mysterious nation.

### Earthquakes — B locally, C as systemic cause
Tectonic damage is plausible at specific sites; synchronized “earthquake storm” causation is disputed.

### Epidemic disease — D as broad collapse driver
Pathogens existed, but direct evidence tying a great epidemic to the entire Late Bronze Age collapse is weak.

### Systems collapse — B as explanatory framework
Interdependent palace economies and prestige networks can become fragile when several shocks coincide, but “complexity causes collapse” is too generic unless specific dependencies are modeled.

### Class revolt/internal rebellion — C
Social conflict is plausible and some destructions may be internal, but direct evidence is generally insufficient to make a universal peasants-versus-palaces story.

---

## 79. Why the Hittite collapse should be uneven

Zooarchaeological work at Hittite sites finds continuity in animal economies at some locations even after political collapse, while others are heavily affected. [R50]

### Simulation
When Hattusa loses central coordination:

- local villages do not instantly disappear;
- herders keep herding if pasture remains;
- officials lose salaries/authority;
- stored palace goods are redistributed, looted or abandoned;
- vassals become independent;
- trade routes reorient;
- local cults continue or change;
- refugees and soldiers move.

Political collapse is an **occupation change for thousands of individuals**, not a global death event.

---

## 80. Mycenaean palace failure

Linear B bureaucracy is exceptionally vulnerable to palace destruction because writing is tied to palace administration. When palaces disappear, the writing system itself disappears from the archaeological record even though people, language, farming, religion and craft traditions continue.

This is a powerful simulation example of **institutional knowledge loss**:

`institution dies → specialist labor market disappears → scribal training stops → technology becomes useless → literacy vanishes locally.`

---

## 81. Ugarit as network-node failure

Ugarit combines:

- palace government;
- maritime trade;
- dependency on regional security;
- multilingual diplomacy;
- agricultural hinterland;
- proximity to great-power conflict.

A model can test whether destruction becomes likely only when several pressures align: reduced grain, missing troops, enemy ships/raiders, failed alliances, and regional trade disruption.

---

## 82. Egypt: survival with contraction

Egypt defeats major external attacks under Ramesses III according to its own monumental record but subsequently experiences fiscal, political and administrative strain. Deir el-Medina's documented wage problems and strikes illustrate institutional stress in the later New Kingdom.

### Simulation lesson
“Survive collapse” can mean **state continuity with reduced imperial reach and declining capacity**, not business as usual.

---

## 83. Post-collapse innovation and adaptation

Collapse removes some institutions and creates opportunity:

- smaller polities;
- new trade routes;
- local craft systems;
- migration and cultural hybridization;
- new political identities;
- eventually alphabetic writing systems expanding in importance;
- changing military organization;
- iron adoption over time.

Do not end the simulation at 1177 BCE as a game-over. Transition is historically more interesting than collapse itself.

## 88. Decision model

A historically plausible agent does not maximize money.

Use a weighted utility function:

`U(action) = survival + household welfare + status/reputation + reciprocity + ritual security + material gain + autonomy + affection - legal risk - supernatural risk - social shame - retaliation risk`.

Weights vary by agent and circumstance.

### Example: farmer hiding grain from tax collector
Benefits: household survives winter, seed preserved.
Costs: legal punishment, patron anger, ritual/oath concern, neighbor denunciation.

### Example: ruler sends extravagant diplomatic gift
Material cost may be high, but parity/reputation benefits can dominate.

### Example: widow challenges inheritance
Potential gain includes property and children's future; costs include kin rupture and legal expense. If local law allows women to litigate, action probability rises.

---

## 89. Social norms as expectations, not mind control

Represent norms as:

`expected_behavior + enforcement_probability + sanction_type + internalization`.

Sanctions:

- gossip;
- loss of marriage prospects;
- refusal of aid;
- compensation;
- exclusion from feast/cult;
- patron withdrawal;
- court penalty;
- divine fear;
- violence.

An agent can violate a norm if incentives are strong enough.

---

## 90. Reputation and gossip

Small settlements create high social observability. Reputation should travel through kin and work networks.

Possible reputations:

- generous host;
- oath-breaker;
- coward;
- skilled weaver;
- reliable merchant;
- dangerous debtor;
- fertile household;
- favored by god;
- unlucky;
- adulterer;
- good caregiver;
- corrupt official;
- brave soldier;
- foreigner who honors customs.

Bronze Age letters and lawsuits make interpersonal reputation a better simulation driver than abstract “morality points.”

---

## 91. Reciprocity ledger

Every gift or favor should create culturally scaled expectations.

Examples:

- food in famine;
- marriage gift;
- diplomatic gold;
- herd access;
- legal witness;
- military support;
- funeral participation;
- old-age care;
- caravan capital;
- priestly service.

A delayed return may be acceptable; refusal can become insult or relationship rupture.

---

## 92. Patron-client dynamics

Patrons provide:

- food/land;
- legal protection;
- office;
- credit;
- access to rulers;
- military backing.

Clients provide:

- labor;
- votes/assembly support where relevant;
- tribute;
- armed service;
- loyalty;
- information;
- public honor.

Dependence can be affectionate, exploitative, strategic, or all three.

---

## 93. Ritual decision engine

When an agent experiences uncertainty:

1. determine whether event belongs to practical, social, supernatural or mixed causal category;
2. choose specialist based on cost/trust/status;
3. perform divination/ritual with stochastic interpreted result;
4. update confidence;
5. act.

Important: divination is not random flavor text. It changes behavior and can coordinate groups.

---

## 94. Language and translation

Characters should have proficiency levels, not binary language flags.

Late Bronze Age diplomatic/scribal agents may use Akkadian as an international written language while speaking another native language. Ugarit is highly multilingual. Mycenaean Greek is written in Linear B only in a restricted administrative context. Indus spoken languages are unknown. Shang Old Chinese reconstruction is partial.

### Dialogue policy
Use modern English for accessibility while generating **socially appropriate concepts**, not fake archaic English. Avoid “thee/thou Bronze Age” dialogue.

---

## 95. Naming policy

Use names securely attested for text-rich regions and periods. For non-literate cultures, avoid inventing pseudo-authentic names presented as fact.

Recommended modes:

- `attested_names` for Mesopotamia, Egypt, Hittite, Ugarit, Mycenaean, Shang;
- `reconstructed_language_names` only when linguistically defensible and labeled;
- `simulation_alias` for archaeological cultures whose personal names are unknown.

---

## 96. Memory and identity

Agents should remember:

- famine years;
- raids;
- betrayal;
- marriage obligations;
- inherited feuds;
- parent stories;
- extraordinary omens;
- ruler generosity;
- displacement;
- debt humiliation;
- successful migration.

Households transmit stories across generations. Collective memory can outlive the event and shape future risk tolerance.

# Part IX — Culture-specific parameter cards

These are starting priors, not deterministic “national traits.”

## 97. Old Babylonian urban card

- **Authority:** king + city institutions + local notables + courts.
- **Household:** strong property/contract/inheritance role.
- **Economy:** irrigation farming, silver/barley credit, merchants, temple/palace plus private sector.
- **Literacy:** specialist but dense documentation among elites/merchants.
- **Religion:** omen/divination/temple/household practice integrated.
- **Social pressure:** reciprocity among peers; debt; legal documentation; status anxiety.
- **Outsider contact:** high in trade centers.
- **Evidence confidence:** high relative to most Bronze Age cultures.

## 98. Old Assyrian merchant card

- **Authority:** home-city institutions + host Anatolian rulers.
- **Household:** transregional family firm.
- **Economy:** tin/textile/silver trade, caravan credit.
- **Risk:** route closure, toll, partner default, donkey loss, political dispute.
- **Social pressure:** kin trust and written partnership.

## 99. Hittite imperial card

- **Authority:** king, royal family, vassals, governors, local communities.
- **Economy:** mixed local staples + elite/state extraction and prestige systems.
- **Religion:** ritual calendar and oath legitimacy extremely salient.
- **Law:** compensation and contractual pragmatism alongside ritual rules.
- **Risk:** succession, frontier war, crop failure, vassal defection.

## 100. Ugaritic port card

- **Authority:** king/palace + households + merchants.
- **Literacy/language:** unusually high scribal diversity.
- **Economy:** agriculture + maritime commerce.
- **Religion:** state + household/family cult.
- **Risk:** international war, shipping disruption, drought, great-power politics.

## 101. New Kingdom Egyptian village card

- **Authority:** state/temple/officials; local dispute mechanisms.
- **Household:** property/inheritance meaningful; women can transact/litigate.
- **Economy:** grain, rations/tax, specialized labor depending site.
- **Religion:** temple, household, ancestor, oracle, magic-medical overlap.
- **Risk:** Nile failure, delayed rations, official corruption, family dispute.

## 102. Kerma/Nubian card

- **Authority:** local king/elites + cattle prestige; later Egyptian imperial layer depending date.
- **Economy:** river agriculture/trade + pastoralism + gold/routes.
- **Identity:** frontier bilingual/cultural mixing possible.
- **Risk:** Egyptian expansion, cattle loss, route disruption.

## 103. Minoan card

- **Authority:** uncertain/center-specific palatial networks.
- **Economy:** mixed agriculture, craft, maritime trade.
- **Religion:** landscape/peak/cave/shrine ritual.
- **Literacy:** Linear A restricted and unreadable to us.
- **Risk:** earthquake/local political competition/trade; avoid “peaceful matriarchy” defaults.

## 104. Mycenaean palatial card

- **Authority:** palace ruler/officials + local landholders.
- **Economy:** household agriculture + substantial palace management of select sectors.
- **Literacy:** narrow Linear B bureaucracy.
- **Social pressure:** obligation, feasting/prestige, military service.
- **Risk:** palace destruction produces institutional knowledge loss.

## 105. Harappan urban card

- **Authority:** deliberately uncertain; choose scenario.
- **Economy:** standardized weights/craft/trade + diverse agriculture.
- **Infrastructure:** water/drainage/planned urban systems.
- **Literacy:** signs exist, language unreadable.
- **Social pressure:** model household/craft/neighborhood; avoid invented kings/priests.
- **Risk:** monsoon/river change, disease, trade shift, urban decentralization.

## 106. BMAC oasis card

- **Authority:** hierarchical center but exact form uncertain.
- **Economy:** irrigation agriculture + craft + long-distance exchange.
- **Frontier:** intense mobile-pastoral interaction.
- **Risk:** water, migration, route shifts.

## 107. Sintashta/steppe card

- **Authority:** kin/prestige and fortified settlement leadership; no empire assumption.
- **Economy:** herding + metallurgy + some agriculture/trade.
- **Prestige:** horses/chariot/weapon/feast in select contexts.
- **Risk:** pasture, herd disease, feud/raid, metal access.

## 108. Shang card

- **Authority:** king + royal/affinal lineages + allies and coercive campaigns.
- **Economy:** millet farming + institutional workshops and provisioning.
- **Religion:** ancestor/divination central at elite level.
- **Violence:** warfare and sacrifice institutionally real.
- **Risk:** rebellious allies, harvest/weather, succession, campaign failure.

## 109. Sanxingdui card

- **Authority:** complex center; exact offices unknown.
- **Economy:** mixed rice/millet, livestock, craft, regional exchange.
- **Religion:** spectacular ritual deposition; theology unknown.
- **Risk:** do not fill textual gaps with Shang assumptions.

## 110. Únětice/Leubingen card

- **Authority:** chiefdom/ranked local system.
- **Household:** patrilineal/virilocal prior at Leubingen; female exogamy.
- **Economy:** farming surplus + craft/exchange.
- **Risk:** lineage competition and trade prestige.

## 111. Mokrin card

- **Authority:** hierarchy less rigidly mapped to marriage.
- **Household:** female exogamy without strict patrilocality.
- **Status:** women can inherit; males can acquire status.
- **Simulation use:** strong anti-stereotype comparison with Leubingen.

## 112. El Argar card

- **Authority:** centralized/hierarchical early-state-like regional system.
- **Household:** virilocal/patrilineal, reciprocal female exogamy.
- **Inequality:** high and durable.
- **Risk:** class extraction, food access, elite coercion.

## 113. Terramare card

- **Authority:** dense community/settlement coordination.
- **Economy:** intensive agriculture, infrastructure, population concentration.
- **Risk:** land pressure + drying + food production + labor maintenance.

# Part XI — Scenario designs for emergent research

## 118. “A normal year” baseline

Before simulating collapse, run hundreds of ordinary years. Test whether:

- households reproduce;
- debt remains plausible;
- food stores cycle seasonally;
- elite extraction is sustainable;
- marriages create realistic kin networks;
- merchants profit without infinite growth;
- ritual consumes real resources;
- random shocks do not cause constant apocalypse.

A collapse model is meaningless if normal society is unstable by construction.

---

## 119. Old Babylonian neighborhood scenario

### Map
20–60 households around canal/market/temple edge.

### Agents
farmers, merchants, scribes, craft workers, priestly household, debtors, dependents.

### Research questions
- When does reciprocal aid prevent debt bondage?
- Does written documentation reduce feud but increase elite advantage?
- How does canal inequality produce patronage?
- What reputations emerge among merchant networks?

---

## 120. Ugarit 20-year crisis scenario

### Inputs
Variable rainfall, grain stock, shipping loss, Hittite war demand, enemy-ship rumors, palace extraction, merchant confidence.

### Observe
- grain prices;
- migration;
- ship departures;
- elite legitimacy;
- whether destruction requires multiple simultaneous failures.

---

## 121. Hittite frontier scenario

Hittite governor + local villages + Kaska communities + seasonal army.

Test whether tribute/raiding cycles stabilize into negotiated coexistence or escalate into depopulation.

---

## 122. Mycenaean palace knowledge-loss scenario

Destroy palace at random year.

Track:

- scribes seeking new occupations;
- loss of Linear B training;
- local craft survival;
- landholder autonomy;
- religious continuity;
- migration;
- whether former palace dependents become vulnerable or independent.

---

## 123. Indus governance uncertainty experiment

Run the same urban population under four institutional hypotheses:

1. centralized ruler bureaucracy;
2. merchant/corporate oligarchy;
3. neighborhood/assembly coordination;
4. ritual-civic distributed authority.

Give all models the same archaeology-constrained outcomes—standardized weights, water infrastructure, craft specialization—and ask which institutions produce those outcomes with least unsupported assumptions.

This is a productive use of simulation: **compare hypotheses rather than pretending to know the unknowable.**

---

## 124. Steppe–BMAC contact scenario

Agents:

- oasis farmers;
- fortress elites;
- pastoral households;
- metalworkers;
- marriage brokers;
- caravan merchants.

Test diffusion of crops, horses, rituals and language traits under trade, intermarriage and migration without assuming conquest.

---

## 125. Shang royal decision scenario

Build a royal court with oracle divination.

Choices:

- campaign now or delay;
- sacrifice to which ancestor;
- dispatch allied lineage;
- harvest labor versus war labor;
- react to childbirth/illness omen.

Compare a “ritual is decorative” model against one where divination has real decision authority. The latter should better reproduce the structure visible in oracle bones.

---

## 126. Terramare overshoot scenario

Increase population, infrastructure and agricultural intensification gradually, then add mild drying.

Measure whether collapse emerges from:

- food deficit;
- infrastructure labor deficit;
- migration;
- elite extraction;
- reduced inter-settlement mobility;
- combinations.

---

## 127. Late Bronze Age network experiment

Nodes:
Hatti, Ugarit, Cyprus, Egyptian ports, Mycenaean polities, Levantine cities, Babylonia/Assyria connections.

Flows:
- copper;
- tin;
- grain;
- timber;
- luxury goods;
- diplomats;
- information;
- mercenaries/migrants.

Randomly shock nodes and edges. Ask which combinations produce:

- price spikes only;
- political realignment;
- migration;
- state collapse;
- recovery through rerouting.

Compare output to archaeology without tuning solely for a 1200 BCE catastrophe.

## 129. Emotional texture examples

Do not write Bronze Age agents as flat ceremonial robots.

Plausible emotional states directly supported by letters/legal archives include:

- anxiety over status;
- resentment at an unreturned favor;
- worry for sick family;

## 130. Avoid modern individualism without erasing individuality

Bad simulation:
> “I want to follow my dream, regardless of my family.”

Not impossible, but it should be socially costly where household duty is strong.

Better decision structure:
> “I want the caravan job, but my father needs me for harvest; my sister's marriage payment is due; my uncle can replace my labor if I promise him a share.”

The person has desire; the household makes desire consequential.

# Part XIII — Research uncertainty and competing interpretations

## 131. Archaeological culture ≠ ethnicity

This is one of the most important safeguards. Similar pottery can spread through:

- migration;
- intermarriage;
- imitation;
- trade;
- craft learning;
- political affiliation;
- ritual fashion.

Ancient DNA can show ancestry shifts but still cannot directly identify spoken language or self-identity. [R34]

---

## 132. “Palace economy” is not total command economy

Palaces are archaeologically visible because they store and write. Households and informal markets are less visible. Build a mixed economy unless direct evidence supports stronger control.

---

## 133. “No fortifications” does not equal peace

Conflict may occur through raids, political coercion, interpersonal violence or temporary defenses. Conversely, fortifications can symbolize power or manage floods/animals in addition to military defense.

---

## 134. Rich burial ≠ exact rank

A lavish grave may encode achieved status, inherited status, ritual identity, family competition, age/sex ideology, or exceptional circumstances. Use burial wealth as probabilistic evidence.

---

## 135. Human sacrifice must be locally constrained

Shang evidence strongly supports institutional human sacrifice. Kerma has contexts interpreted as human sacrifice/retainer burial. Other regions do not. Do not turn “Bronze Age religion” into ubiquitous human sacrifice.

---

## 136. The Indus script must remain undeciphered

Any simulation that gives “translated Harappan dialogue from inscriptions” is inventing evidence. Spoken dialogue can be generated in modern English for agents, but language names and written meanings must be scenario assumptions.

---

## 137. Minoan matriarchy is unproven

Prominent female imagery and possible goddesses do not establish female political dominance. Allow strong female ritual roles without a blanket matriarchal state.

---

## 138. Homer is not a Mycenaean documentary archive

Later Greek epic may preserve Bronze Age objects, places and social memories, but it was composed/transmitted centuries later. Use Linear B and archaeology first; use Homer as later comparative tradition with explicit uncertainty.

---

## 139. Biblical texts are not simple Late Bronze Age transcripts

Later textual traditions may preserve memories, place names and institutions but were compiled/redacted in later periods. Use contemporary Egyptian, Ugaritic, Hittite and archaeological evidence to reconstruct Late Bronze Age Canaan.

---

## 140. “Sea Peoples caused the collapse” is not an acceptable hard-coded event

Egyptian records support conflicts with named groups. Archaeology supports mobility and cultural change. But identity, scale and causal role remain debated. The best simulation lets raiding/migration emerge from prior instability and then feed back into it. [R48][R49]

# Part XVI — What “exhaustive” still cannot mean

Even a very long encyclopedia cannot recover:

- the spoken language of the Indus civilization with confidence;
- the personal beliefs of an average Minoan farmer;
- the exact political constitution of Sanxingdui;
- a universal Bronze Age childhood schedule;
- the precise ethnic identity of every “Sea Peoples” group;
- a single cause of the c. 1200 BCE transformations;
- the personality distribution of archaeological cultures.

The correct simulation behavior is to expose these as **uncertainty switches** and compare worlds.

# Part XVIII — Research verification notes

## 153. Claims given an independent verification pass

The following high-impact claims were specifically checked against recent or primary-specialist scholarship rather than inherited popular narratives:

- **The 4.2 ka event:** a 2024 global synthesis of 1,142 paleoclimate datasets rejects a globally coherent exceptional event while preserving regional anomalies. [R09]
- **Late Bronze Age collapse:** recent reviews continue to favor heterogeneous, interacting causes and warn against a single-collapse script. [R10][R11][R12]
- **Sea Peoples:** contemporary Egyptian evidence establishes named adversary groups, but their composition, scale and role vary; scholarship does not justify a single invading nation. [R48][R49]
- **Epidemic as collapse driver:** ancient plague is real, but direct evidence for an epidemic causing the eastern Mediterranean collapse is weak; Amarna itself does not show the mortality crisis once claimed. [R23][R47]
- **European household structure:** direct genetic/isotopic studies demonstrate meaningful differences among Leubingen, Mokrin and El Argar, falsifying a one-size-fits-all Bronze Age patrilocality model. [R06][R07][R08]
- **Linear B:** writing was tightly coupled to palatial administration, explaining why institutional collapse could destroy a writing tradition without destroying the underlying spoken language. [R32]
- **Harappan society:** urban coordination and standardization are secure; exact political constitution and script meanings are not. Violence and disease occur, so “peaceful egalitarian Indus” is too strong, while some recent urban evidence is compatible with comparatively low infrastructural inequality. [R37][R38]
- **Sanxingdui:** new 2025–2026 work supports Late Shang-period ritual deposition and refines how the pits formed, replacing older sensational narratives with increasingly constrained archaeological models. [R42][R43]
- **Steppe mobility:** mobile pastoralism is diverse; Kura-Araxes isotope results show that a culture often associated with mobility can include strongly local herd management. [R14]
- **Old Babylonian slavery/dependency:** recent synthesis confirms that unfreedom included debt-related conditional dependency as well as more permanent slavery, requiring a multidimensional rather than binary status system. [R24]

# Part XIX — Source guide and keyed bibliography

The bibliography emphasizes sources that constrain simulation variables or correct common misconceptions. `R##` identifiers correspond to citations used throughout the report. Several entries are synthetic works supplemented by the narrower studies listed beside them.

## Networks, metallurgy, chronology, and method

**R01 — Uluburun metal cargo and tin provenance.** Cemal Pulak and continuing archaeometric work on the c. 14th-century BCE Uluburun shipwreck; recent isotopic provenance studies reconstruct multiple tin-source possibilities and reinforce the scale of eastern Mediterranean metal exchange. See the Uluburun cargo syntheses and the 2022–2024 tin provenance debate.

**R02 — Old Assyrian caravan economy.** Kültepe/Kaneš archives and recent archaeometric studies of tin. The documents describe Assyrian merchant families, donkey caravans, tin/textile movement into Anatolia and silver returning east; metallurgy increasingly constrains source hypotheses.

**R03 — Richardson, Seth. “Old Babylonian Letters and Class Formation.”** *Journal of Ancient History* (2022). Especially valuable for interpersonal reciprocity, peer labels, status anxiety, loneliness and the emotional texture of letters.

**R04 — Amarna diplomatic correspondence.** The Amarna letters and modern diplomatic analyses; central evidence for Great King “brotherhood,” gift reciprocity, marriage diplomacy, vassal appeals and status negotiation.

**R05 — Deir el-Medina family/property records and Naunakhte.** Ostraca and papyri from the royal workmen’s village; Naunakhte’s will is a particularly clear case of inheritance linked to elder-care reciprocity.

## Kinship, household, and status

**R06 — Leubingen kinship.** Early Bronze Age Leubingen genomic/isotopic study, *Scientific Reports* (2024): 46 individuals, five pedigrees, predominantly patrilineal/virilocal structure and female exogamy. This is a local community result, not a Europe-wide rule.

**R07 — Mokrin kinship.** *Scientific Reports* (2021): ancient DNA from the Mokrin necropolis; female exogamy without strict patrilocality, status transmission that could include women, and evidence against rigid universal marriage barriers.

**R08 — El Argar / La Almoloya kinship.** *Scientific Reports* (2022): strongly virilocal/patrilineal pedigrees, reciprocal female exogamy, and possible serial monogamy/polygyny in a markedly unequal society.

**R09 — Degroot et al. / global 4.2 ka assessment.** *Nature Communications* (2024), global synthesis of 1,142 paleoclimate datasets. Key conclusion: no globally coherent, exceptional 4.2 ka climate event; regional dry, wet and null expressions differ. This is the principal source for rejecting a scripted global 2200 BCE megadrought.

## Collapse, climate, and systemic risk

**R10 — Late Bronze Age network fragility model.** *Global Environmental Change* (2024), network-science analysis of eastern Mediterranean Bronze Age interdependence. Useful as a mechanism model: most isolated failures need not cascade, while some combinations can. It is not direct proof of one historical causal chain.

**R11 — Recent reassessments of the end of the Bronze Age.** *Antiquity* and related syntheses, 2023–2025, emphasizing regionally variable destruction, continuity, migration, climate and political change rather than one universal “collapse event.”

**R12 — Middleton and Aegean collapse scholarship.** Oxford and other recent syntheses on Mycenaean palace destruction and post-palatial continuity; particularly important for separating institutional disappearance from population disappearance.

**R13 — Regional chronology and Africa/Southeast Asia.** Oxford/archaeological syntheses on African metallurgy and Southeast Asian archaeometallurgy. The “Bronze Age” category is regionally uneven; many sub-Saharan trajectories do not include a distinct bronze-dominant age, and mainland Southeast Asia enters bronze metallurgy substantially later than Mesopotamia.

**R14 — Kura-Araxes herding at Köhne Shahar.** Isotopic study of animal management showing primarily local herding, limited transhumance and winter foddering. A direct warning against equating Kura-Araxes expansion with long-range pastoral nomadism.

## Law, gender, childhood, and labor

**R15 — Old Babylonian dowry and women’s property.** Legal/archival studies of dowry, seals, weights, divorce and inheritance. Useful for modeling women as economic/legal actors inside a patriarchal household system rather than treating them as propertyless by default.

**R16 — Bryce, Trevor. Studies of Hittite marriage and family law.** Formal and de facto marriage, compensation, prenuptial provisions and divorce—including female-initiated divorce—show flexible legal mechanisms inside a strongly household-oriented society.

**R17 — Mesopotamian childhood.** Archaeological/textual synthesis on children, infancy, socialization, adoption, education and labor. Important because both texts and cemeteries systematically underrepresent children in different ways.

**R18 — Mycenaean women and textile labor.** Linear B research documenting thousands of women in administrative work groups and significant variation between palace centers such as Knossos and Pylos.

**R19 — Nakassis, Dimitri. *Individuals and Society in Mycenaean Pylos*.** Named-person prosopography demonstrating multiplex roles and undermining rigid, single-role Mycenaean class models.

## Subsistence, health, and ordinary life

**R20 — Sanxingdui/Chengdu Plain subsistence.** 2024 archaeological synthesis of mixed rice/millet agriculture, livestock and continued hunting/fishing/gathering, with explicit caution about incomplete archaeobotanical recovery.

**R21 — Must Farm.** Cambridge Archaeological Unit / Historic England research on the c. 850 BCE pile-dwelling settlement: exceptional preservation of architecture, textiles, tools, pottery, food remains and complete household assemblages. Although very late relative to the eastern Mediterranean Bronze Age, it is one of the best windows into prehistoric domestic materiality.

**R22 — Bioarchaeology of Bronze Age health and activity.** Regional osteological/isotopic syntheses used for disease, trauma, nutrition, workload and mobility; interpretation must account for cemetery selection and preservation.

**R23 — Prehistoric plague genomics.** Ancient-DNA studies of *Yersinia pestis*, including a 2026 trans-Eurasian study adding East Asian and Minusinsk Basin lineages. Establishes pathogen presence and dispersal, not a universal collapse role.

**R24 — Richardson, Seth. “Mesopotamian Slavery.”** In *The Palgrave Handbook of Global Slavery throughout History* (Springer, 2023). Particularly useful on Old Babylonian debt-related dependency, redemption, sale/transfer, permanent foreign slavery and the inadequacy of a simple free/slave binary. Publisher page: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-13260-5_2

**R25 — Charpin, Dominique. *Writing, Law, and Kingship in Old Babylonian Mesopotamia*.** Essential for archives, deeds, courts, scribes, contracts and the practical relationship between writing and legal security.

**R26 — Hoffner, Harry A. *The Laws of the Hittites: A Critical Edition*.** Core source for compensation, injury, property, sex, marriage, livestock, slaves and legal categories.

**R27 — Deir el-Medina legal records.** Specialized studies of disputes and documentation showing the archive’s heavy bias toward private economic/property matters. This supports the warning that archival absence does not equal social absence.

**R28 — Shang oracle-bone corpora and modern syntheses.** Direct evidence for royal divination concerning rain, harvest, campaigns, sacrifice, childbirth, illness and ancestors; a uniquely rich but socially elite-biased behavioral archive.

**R29 — Deir el-Medina oracles.** Studies of the oracle of Amenhotep I and local legal/religious practice, including women’s consultation over property and household problems.

**R30 — Deir el-Medina private letters.** Family/friend correspondence preserving individual voice, interpersonal conflict, emotion and household concerns.

## Trade, writing, and interstate systems

**R31 — Kültepe/Kaneš tin and merchants.** Old Assyrian texts plus recent tin-isotope studies; direct support for trans-Anatolian caravan organization and evidence that metal provenance is more complicated than a one-mine story.

**R32 — Linear B scribal practice and institutional restriction.** Anna P. Judson, “Learning to Spell in Linear B: Orthography and Scribal Training in Mycenaean Pylos,” *Cambridge Classical Journal* 68 (2022), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S1750270522000057; Judson, “The Tablet-Makers of Pylos,” *Annual of the British School at Athens* (2023); and recent writing-systems synthesis emphasizing Linear B’s narrow administrative use and vulnerability when palace bureaucracies disappeared.

**R33 — Sintashta chariots and metallurgy.** Archaeological syntheses of fortified Sintashta settlements, horse/chariot burials and metal production. Strong evidence for specialist chariot technology; much weaker basis for treating all steppe residents as chariot warriors.

**R34 — Bronze Age Eurasian archaeogenetics.** Allentoft et al., “Population genomics of Bronze Age Eurasia,” *Nature* (2015), plus subsequent kinship studies. Foundational evidence for major migration and ancestry change; should be paired with the methodological warning that ancestry, language, material culture and identity are not synonyms.

**R35 — Ancient state economies.** *Annual Review of Anthropology*, “Archaeology of Ancient State Economies” and later economic syntheses. Key corrective to a total-command “palace economy”: household production, institutional extraction, markets, credit and private exchange coexist in varying proportions.

**R36 — Mittani and Central Asian state formation.** Eva von Dassow, “Mittani and Its Empire,” in *The Oxford History of the Ancient Near East, Vol. III* (2022), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190687601.003.0029, paired with specialist BMAC/Oxus syntheses. Mittani’s upper-Khabur core, Hurrian majority, Indo-Aryan elite elements, chariotry and diverse imperial models are much better supported than a simple “Indo-Aryan conquest caste” narrative.

## Indus and China

**R37 — Indus script and political uncertainty.** Current Indus epigraphy and archaeological syntheses: short undeciphered sign sequences, no accepted bilingual key, and no securely identified royal institution. This constrains simulation naming, law and theology more strongly than popular reconstructions usually acknowledge.

**R38 — Harappan inequality, disease and violence.** Bioarchaeological work at Harappa demonstrating socially uneven infectious disease and interpersonal trauma, paired with a 2026 Mohenjo-daro study arguing comparatively low residential/infrastructural inequality in sampled urban sectors. Together they argue for multidimensional, site-specific inequality rather than either “egalitarian utopia” or “despotic empire.”

**R39 — Tarim Basin ancient DNA.** Early Bronze Age Tarim-mummy genomic research showing substantial local ancestry despite participation in broad cultural/technological networks. Strong evidence that cultural diffusion does not require demographic replacement.

**R40 — *The Shang Economy*.** Cambridge University Press synthesis (2025) on domestic and institutional production, specialization, distribution and commercialization, with explicit discussion of evidentiary limitations.

**R41 — Campbell, Roderick. *Violence, Kinship and the Early Chinese State* / related Shang sovereignty studies.** Models Shang power through royal kin networks, campaigns, sacrifice, boundaries and personal mobilization rather than a modern homogeneous territorial bureaucracy.

**R42 — Sanxingdui pits K3–K8, 2025 dating/synthesis.** New excavation program reporting more than 17,000 artifacts/materials including bronze, jade, gold, ivory and silk remnants, placing the major deposits broadly in the Late Shang period and strengthening ritual/sacrificial interpretations.

**R43 — Sanxingdui Pit K4 geoarchaeology.** *Journal of Archaeological Science* (2026): refined depositional sequence and evidence that burned residues were introduced from external fires rather than proving the objects burned in situ. Important demonstration of how new microstratigraphic work can overturn dramatic site narratives.

## Caucasus and Europe

**R44 — South Caucasus Early Bronze Age / kurgan transformations.** Regional syntheses of Kura-Araxes agro-pastoral households and later rich kurgan traditions, emphasizing changing rank, mobility and prestige rather than a single ethnic succession story.

**R45 — Late Bronze Age South Caucasus fortress polities.** Recent work demonstrating complex political institutions among mobile pastoral populations, with fortresses, feasting and ritual anchoring power despite limited urbanism.

**R46 — MALTHUS / Terramare project, University of Bologna (2023–2026).** Multidisciplinary work on Terramare demography, diet, mobility, climate and collapse. Findings reported to date support changing nutrition/mobility and warming/drying around 1200 BCE while finding no pathogen signal sufficient to explain abandonment. Useful for an endogenous overshoot-and-shock simulation.

**R47 — “A Mortality Crisis at Akhetaten?”** *American Journal of Archaeology* reassessment of skeletal evidence from Amarna; finds insufficient evidence for the catastrophic epidemic often asserted for the city. The Hittite textual “plague” remains real evidence of disease crisis elsewhere, but its pathogen is not securely identified.

## Sea Peoples, migration, and collapse interpretation

**R48 — Sea Peoples terminology and social composition.** *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* (2022) study of Egyptian terminology for Sea Peoples leaders; supports interpreting at least some groups as relatively small warrior/raider formations rather than automatically as entire migrating populations. This complements, rather than eliminates, evidence for migration/family movement in other contexts.

**R49 — Knapp, A. Bernard. *Migration Myths and the End of the Bronze Age in the Eastern Mediterranean*.** Major critical synthesis warning against mapping archaeological assemblages directly to migrating ethnic “peoples” and against circular narratives of invasion.

**R50 — Hittite collapse zooarchaeology.** 2022 *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* research comparing animal economies at Hittite sites across political collapse; demonstrates meaningful local continuity at some settlements and heavier disruption at others. This is central to modeling state failure separately from household subsistence failure.

# Part XX — A practical historical-simulation doctrine

## 154. Ten rules to preserve historical plausibility

1. **Generate people from households and roles, not civilizations.**
2. **Give every cultural rule a date and geography.**
3. **Make social expectations costly but violable.**
4. **Let practical and supernatural causal models coexist.**
5. **Model material bottlenecks—food, water, labor, animals, fuel, transport—not just money.**
6. **Make information slow, lossy and political.**
7. **Keep states spatially uneven; a royal edict is not automatic village behavior.**
8. **Let migration, trade, intermarriage and coercion create mixed identities.**
9. **Model collapse as changing institutions, occupations and networks, not population deletion.**
10. **Where historians disagree, run competing models instead of silently choosing the most dramatic story.**

---

## 155. The minimum viable believable Bronze Age character

A good simulated person needs at least:

- a real place and year;
- household membership;
- age/sex/life stage;
- legal/status position;
- two or more work roles;
- food/resource position;
- three to ten meaningful relationships;
- obligations owed and due;
- local ritual beliefs;
- individual disposition;
- remembered experiences;
- imperfect information;
- a reason to care about the next season;
- a reason to care about what other people think.

A character becomes historically interesting when those dimensions conflict.

**Example:** A 31-year-old Old Assyrian merchant in Kaneš hears that a route is unsafe. His brother in Ashur expects silver; his Anatolian partner owes him money; two donkeys are sick; his daughter's marriage contribution is due; an official has raised a toll; another caravan claims the danger is exaggerated. His choice is not “trade or don't trade.” It is a weighted conflict among household obligation, reputation, credit maturity, animal welfare, political risk and imperfect news.

**Example:** A Mycenaean textile supervisor shortly before palace destruction is responsible for dependent work groups and quota delivery. Her family is local, but her livelihood is institutionally attached. If the palace burns, she does not become “post-apocalyptic”; she loses the administrative system that defined quotas, rations and perhaps status, and must renegotiate land, labor and patronage with surviving households.

**Example:** A Shang lineage leader faces a royal call for campaign manpower during an uncertain harvest. Refusing risks political punishment and loss of royal favor; complying risks household labor shortage. An oracle result may alter the perceived supernatural risk but does not erase the material tradeoff.

# Most important sources

1. **The Oxford History of the Ancient Near East, Vol. III (2022).** The strongest broad scholarly anchor for the Late Bronze Age Near East because it integrates political, social, administrative, religious and interregional evidence while keeping regional differences visible. [R36]
2. **The 2024 global 4.2 ka paleoclimate synthesis.** Crucial because it directly overturns one of the most tempting simulation mistakes: a synchronized global drought switch. [R09]
3. **Leubingen, Mokrin and El Argar archaeogenetic/isotopic kinship studies.** Together they demonstrate empirically that marriage residence, descent and status inheritance vary between Bronze Age communities rather than following one universal patriarchal household template. [R06][R07][R08]
4. **Old Babylonian private letters and legal archives.** Among the best evidence anywhere in the Bronze Age for interpersonal behavior, reciprocity, anxiety, debt, family property and everyday conflict. [R03][R15][R24][R25]
5. **Deir el-Medina letters, work records and legal texts.** Exceptional evidence for family emotion, women’s property actions, wages/rations, gossip, disputes, caregiving, religion and individual voices. [R05][R27][R29][R30]
6. **Linear B administrative scholarship.** Directly constrains Mycenaean labor, institutions and literacy, and provides a rare case where institutional collapse can be connected to loss of a writing system. [R18][R19][R32]
7. **Indus archaeology and bioarchaeology.** Essential precisely because it demonstrates how much a simulation must leave uncertain when writing is undeciphered: urban organization is visible; political offices and theology are not. [R37][R38]
8. **Shang oracle bones and Anyang archaeology.** The strongest direct window into Bronze Age royal uncertainty-management, ancestor politics, warfare and sacrifice in East Asia. [R28][R40][R41]
9. **Uluburun and Kültepe/Kaneš.** Together they make long-distance connectivity concrete: metals, merchants, transport animals/ships, credit, weights, foreign goods and politically contingent routes. [R01][R02][R31]
10. **Recent Late Bronze Age collapse and migration reassessments.** These provide the strongest guardrail against a scripted “Sea Peoples destroy civilization” model and support heterogeneous interacting mechanisms. [R10][R11][R12][R48][R49][R50]

# Remaining uncertainties

- **Indus political organization and language remain fundamentally unresolved.** No simulation can recover exact titles, dynasties, spoken language or theology from current evidence. Governance must remain a scenario variable. [R37]
- **Minoan political organization, language and much theology remain obscure** because Linear A is undeciphered and iconography is open to multiple readings.
- **Sanxingdui political offices and theology remain largely archaeologically reconstructed.** New excavation is rapidly improving chronology and ritual context but cannot yet provide native narrative texts. [R42][R43]
- **Mittani’s internal administration and court are incompletely known** because its own central royal archives have not been recovered; much reconstruction comes from peripheral and foreign documentation. [R36]
- **The social identities behind many archaeological “cultures” remain uncertain.** Ancient DNA improves ancestry and kinship reconstruction but does not directly reveal language, ethnic self-identification or political affiliation. [R34]
- **Population, fertility, mortality and household-size estimates are often too coarse for civilization-wide constants.** A simulation should use broad priors calibrated at site/region level rather than false precision.
- **The relative causal weights in the c. 1200 BCE transformations remain unresolved.** Climate, food stress, warfare, political conflict, migration, maritime insecurity, earthquakes and network fragility probably mattered differently by location. [R10][R11][R12]
- **The identity and organization of Sea Peoples groups remain contested.** Some evidence fits raiding/warrior groups, some migration and family movement, and some service as mercenaries; these categories can overlap. [R48][R49]
- **Disease burden is poorly recoverable where pathogen DNA does not preserve.** Ancient plague is established, but absence of detected pathogen DNA is not proof of absence, and presence does not prove political causation. [R23][R47]
- **Ordinary emotions and beliefs are unevenly visible.** Text-rich societies provide extraordinary evidence for some literate households; non-literate communities require cautious archaeological/comparative reconstruction.
- **Trade provenance remains active science.** Tin in particular has competing provenance interpretations, and recycling complicates chemical/isotopic source assignment. [R01][R31]

# What could change the conclusion

The central conclusion of this report is methodological: **a historically serious Bronze Age simulation should model culturally and geographically constrained individuals inside households and networks, with explicit uncertainty, rather than assigning fixed psychological traits to civilizations or scripting single-cause collapses.** That conclusion is robust.

It would be modified—not overturned—by discoveries such as:

- decipherment of Linear A or the Indus script, which could radically improve knowledge of institutions, names, religion and social categories;
- discovery of a central Mittani archive or comparably rich indigenous archives in currently text-poor regions;
- large new ancient-DNA/isotope datasets showing that current kinship case studies are atypical;
- pathogen evidence securely connecting a major epidemic to a specific political transformation;
- high-resolution, independently replicated climate records establishing a much more spatially coherent environmental shock than current evidence supports;
- decisive provenance work identifying major Bronze Age tin supply routes and their chronological changes;
- new excavations demonstrating substantially different urban inequality or political organization at Indus or Sanxingdui centers;
- archaeological evidence tightly synchronizing destruction events previously assumed to be separated in time.

For simulation engineering, the safest response to such discoveries is not to redesign the behavioral architecture. It is to update **dated, geographic priors and competing scenario weights**. The household/network/uncertainty architecture is deliberately designed to absorb improved evidence.

## Closing synthesis

The Bronze Age becomes believable when it stops being a parade of kings and bronze swords. It is a world in which a failed rain can alter a marriage, a marriage can connect two political communities, a merchant’s unpaid debt can affect a caravan, a caravan can carry strategic tin, a missing shipment can constrain elite gift exchange, a diplomatic insult can alter an alliance, an alliance can determine whether a border town receives soldiers, and a border war can send migrants into another community whose harvest is already poor.

At the human scale, people are neither modern individuals in costumes nor prisoners of a monolithic culture. They are situated actors. They love relatives and resent them; bargain, lie, worship, doubt interpreters, remember favors, seek prestige, fear hunger, care for children, exploit dependents, migrate, improvise, and sometimes violate the rules they publicly affirm. What makes them historically Bronze Age is not a different species of mind. It is the **structure of choices around them**: household property, lineage, ecology, labor calendar, legal status, gods and ancestors, slow information, embodied craft knowledge, political extraction, gift reciprocity, mobility, and material bottlenecks.

That is the level at which a simulation can be both dramatically alive and archaeologically disciplined.
